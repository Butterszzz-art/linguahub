LESSON 10: Greetings and Good-byes
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Greetings are the first thing you'll actually use in real conversation, and
Italian has a clear formal/informal split that echoes the tu/Lei distinction
from Lesson 7. This lesson covers hellos, good-byes, and how to reply
naturally when someone greets you.

GRAMMAR:
- Greetings shift by time of day and by formality level, just like the
  verbs used to address people:
    Informal, any time: ciao (hi/bye) — use only with tu-level relationships
    Formal, morning/early afternoon: buongiorno (good morning/good day)
    Formal, evening: buonasera (good evening)
    Before bed: buonanotte (good night) — a farewell, not a greeting
- Good-byes: arrivederci (informal-to-neutral, "until we see each other
  again") and arrivederLa (the extra-formal version, addressed to a single
  person you're speaking to with Lei). A ciao works for informal partings
  too.
- Replying to "come stai/sta?" (how are you?): Italians commonly answer
  with a scale rather than a flat yes/no — bene (well), non c'è male (not
  bad), così così (so-so), male (badly) — then often return the question
  with "e tu?" / "e Lei?" (and you?).

VOCABULARY:
ciao (chow) - hi, bye (informal)
buongiorno (bwohn-JOHR-noh) - good morning, good day
buonasera (bwoh-nah-SEH-rah) - good evening
buonanotte (bwoh-nah-NOHT-teh) - good night
arrivederci (ahr-ree-veh-DEHR-chee) - goodbye
arrivederLa (ahr-ree-veh-DEHR-lah) - goodbye (very formal)
come stai/sta? (KOH-meh STAH-ee/stah) - how are you? (inf./form.)
non c'è male (nohn cheh MAH-leh) - not bad
così così (koh-ZEE koh-ZEE) - so-so
a presto (ah PREHS-toh) - see you soon

EXAMPLES:
Buongiorno, signora Rossi! Come sta? - bwohn-JOHR-noh, see-NYOH-rah
  ROHS-see! KOH-meh stah - Good morning, Mrs. Rossi! How are you?
Ciao Marco, come stai? Non c'è male, e tu? - chow MAHR-koh, KOH-meh
  STAH-ee? nohn cheh MAH-leh, eh too - Hi Marco, how are you? Not bad,
  and you?
È tardi, buonanotte a tutti! - eh TAHR-dee, bwoh-nah-NOHT-teh ah TOOT-tee -
  It's late, good night everyone!
Arrivederci, a presto! - ahr-ree-veh-DEHR-chee, ah PREHS-toh - Goodbye,
  see you soon!

NOTES:
- Buonanotte is exclusively a farewell (said when parting for the night),
  never a greeting — unlike buongiorno/buonasera, which work as both hello
  and general well-wishes throughout their time of day.
- Match your greeting's formality to your interlocutor: ciao with a
  shopkeeper you've just met can come across as too casual; buongiorno is
  the safer opener with strangers.

PRACTICE:
Write the right greeting for each: entering a bakery at 9am; leaving a
friend's party at 1am; saying hello to your boss at 4pm; texting a close
friend "hi, how are you?"

LESSON 11: Making Introductions
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Once you can greet someone, the natural next step is introducing yourself
and others. This lesson covers names, the verb chiamarsi (to be called),
and polite phrases for meeting someone for the first time.

GRAMMAR:
- To say your name, Italian uses the reflexive verb chiamarsi (literally
  "to call oneself"): Mi chiamo Anna. (My name is Anna. / I call myself
  Anna.) You'll cover reflexive verbs fully in Lesson 31, but this common
  phrase is worth learning now.
    io mi chiamo / tu ti chiami / lui-lei si chiama / noi ci chiamiamo /
    voi vi chiamate / loro si chiamano
- To ask someone's name: Come ti chiami? (informal) / Come si chiama?
  (formal)
- To introduce someone else, use the verb presentare (to present/
  introduce): Ti presento Marco. (Let me introduce you to Marco. — lit.
  "I present Marco to you.") Formally: Le presento il signor Bianchi.
- Standard responses when meeting someone: piacere (pleasure — the Italian
  equivalent of "nice to meet you") and molto lieto/lieta (delighted,
  m./f.).

VOCABULARY:
chiamarsi (kyah-MAHR-see) - to be called, named
mi chiamo (mee KYAH-moh) - my name is
come ti chiami? (KOH-meh tee KYAH-mee) - what's your name? (informal)
come si chiama? (KOH-meh see KYAH-mah) - what's your name? (formal)
presentare (preh-zehn-TAH-reh) - to introduce, present
piacere (pyah-CHEH-reh) - pleasure, nice to meet you
molto lieto/lieta (MOHL-toh LYEH-toh/LYEH-tah) - delighted (m./f.)
questo/questa è ... (KWEHS-toh/KWEHS-tah eh) - this is ...
il signore/la signora (eel see-NYOH-reh/lah see-NYOH-rah) - Mr./Mrs.
la signorina (lah see-nyoh-REE-nah) - Miss

EXAMPLES:
Ciao, come ti chiami? Mi chiamo Sofia. - chow, KOH-meh tee KYAH-mee? mee
  KYAH-moh SOH-fyah - Hi, what's your name? My name is Sofia.
Piacere di conoscerLa. - pyah-CHEH-reh dee koh-NOH-shehr-lah - A pleasure
  to meet you. (formal)
Ti presento la mia amica Giulia. - tee preh-ZEHN-toh lah MEE-ah ah-MEE-kah
  JOOL-yah - Let me introduce you to my friend Giulia.
Questo è mio fratello, Luca. - KWEHS-toh eh MEE-oh frah-TEHL-loh, LOO-kah -
  This is my brother, Luca.

NOTES:
- "Piacere" alone is enough as a first response when meeting someone — you
  don't need a full sentence to be polite.
- Match the formal or informal introduction phrase to the person you're
  addressing (Le presento... vs. Ti presento...), the same way you choose
  between Lei and tu.

PRACTICE:
Introduce yourself and then introduce a friend to a new acquaintance,
first informally (with a peer) and then formally (with your friend's boss).

LESSON 12: Getting Acquainted — Where Are You From?
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Small talk about origins and language ability is some of the most useful
early conversation you'll have in Italian. This lesson covers asking and
answering where someone is from, and whether they speak a language.

GRAMMAR:
- To ask where someone is from, use the verb essere (to be) + di (from) or
  the phrase venire da (to come from):
    Di dove sei? / Di dov'è? (Where are you from? inf./form.)
    Sono di New York. (I'm from New York.)
    Da dove vieni? / Da dove viene? (Where do you come from? inf./form.)
- Country and nationality names are related but not identical — the
  country is usually capitalized, the nationality/language adjective is
  not: l'Italia (Italy) -> italiano (Italian); la Francia (France) ->
  francese (French); gli Stati Uniti (the United States) -> americano
  (American).
- To ask if someone speaks a language, use parlare (Lesson 8): Parla
  inglese? (Do you speak English? formal) / Parli italiano? (informal)
- To talk about ability with a language, you can also use capire
  (understand) and un po' (a little): Capisco un po' d'italiano. (I
  understand a little Italian.)

VOCABULARY:
di dove sei/è? (dee DOH-veh SEY/eh) - where are you from? (inf./form.)
venire (veh-NEE-reh) - to come
l'Italia (lee-TAH-lyah) - Italy
gli Stati Uniti (lyee STAH-tee oo-NEE-tee) - the United States
l'Inghilterra (leen-geel-TEHR-rah) - England
italiano/a (ee-tah-LYAH-noh) - Italian
americano/a (ah-meh-ree-KAH-noh) - American
inglese (een-GLEH-zeh) - English
un po' (oon poh) - a little
molto bene (MOHL-toh BEH-neh) - very well

EXAMPLES:
Di dove sei? Sono americana, di Chicago. - dee DOH-veh SEY? SOH-noh
  ah-meh-ree-KAH-nah, dee shee-KAH-goh - Where are you from? I'm American,
  from Chicago.
Parla inglese? Sì, ma parlo anche italiano. - PAHR-lah een-GLEH-zeh? see,
  mah PAHR-loh AHN-keh ee-tah-LYAH-noh - Do you speak English? Yes, but I
  also speak Italian.
Parlo poco ma capisco di più. - PAHR-loh POH-koh mah kah-PEES-koh dee
  pyoo - I speak a little but understand more.
Da dove vieni? Vengo dalla Francia. - dah DOH-veh VYEH-nee? VEHN-goh
  DAHL-lah FRAHN-chah - Where do you come from? I come from France.

NOTES:
- Country names take a definite article (l'Italia, la Francia, gli Stati
  Uniti), but the article usually drops after the preposition "in" for
  countries: vivo in Italia (not "nell'Italia"), though it's kept for
  plural or modified country names: negli Stati Uniti.
- "Parlo poco ma capisco di più" is a genuinely useful, humble phrase for
  real conversations while you're still learning — Italians respond well
  to it.

PRACTICE:
Answer in full sentences: Di dove sei? Parli italiano? Da dove vengono i
tuoi genitori (your parents)?

LESSON 13: Numbers
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Numbers show up everywhere — prices, phone numbers, ages, addresses — so
this lesson builds the full counting system from zero through the
millions, plus the two irregular quirks worth knowing up front.

GRAMMAR:
- Numbers 0-10: zero, uno, due, tre, quattro, cinque, sei, sette, otto,
  nove, dieci
- Numbers 11-19 are irregular and must be memorized individually: undici,
  dodici, tredici, quattordici, quindici, sedici, diciassette, diciotto,
  diciannove
- From 20 on, numbers are built by combining a "tens" word with a "ones"
  word, exactly like English (twenty-two): venti + due = ventidue.
- When a "tens" word ends in a vowel and meets uno or otto, the tens word
  DROPS its final vowel: venti + uno = ventuno (not ventiuno); quaranta +
  otto = quarantotto (not quarantaotto).
- Tens: venti (20), trenta (30), quaranta (40), cinquanta (50), sessanta
  (60), settanta (70), ottanta (80), novanta (90), cento (100)
- Hundreds simply prefix cento: duecento (200), trecento (300), and so on
  — no space, no "e."
- Thousands: mille (1,000) is irregular in the plural — it becomes -mila:
  duemila (2,000), tremila (3,000). Millions and billions: un milione
  (1,000,000, note the different word), un miliardo (1,000,000,000).
- Decades as a period of time ("the sixties") need a phrase, not a single
  word: gli anni sessanta (literally "the years sixty").

VOCABULARY:
zero (DZEH-roh) - zero
uno-dieci (OO-noh-DYEH-chee) - one-ten
undici-diciannove (OON-dee-chee-dee-chahn-NOH-veh) - eleven-nineteen
venti (VEHN-tee) - twenty
trenta (TREHN-tah) - thirty
cento (CHEHN-toh) - one hundred
mille (MEEL-leh) - one thousand
un milione (oon mee-LYOH-neh) - one million
quanti anni hai? (KWAHN-tee AHN-nee AH-ee) - how old are you?
numero di telefono (NOO-meh-roh dee teh-LEH-foh-noh) - phone number

EXAMPLES:
Ho trentatré anni. - oh trehn-tah-TREH AHN-nee - I'm thirty-three years
  old.
Il biglietto costa quarantotto euro. - eel bee-LYEHT-toh KOHS-tah
  kwah-rahn-TOHT-toh EH-oo-roh - The ticket costs forty-eight euros.
Ci sono duemilaventisei studenti. - chee SOH-noh doo-eh-mee-lah-vehn-tee-
  SEY stoo-DEHN-tee - There are two thousand and twenty-six students.
Il mio numero è zero-tre-tre... - eel MEE-oh NOO-meh-roh eh DZEH-roh treh
  treh - My number is oh-three-three...

NOTES:
- The uno/otto vowel-drop rule (ventuno, trentotto, quarantuno,
  cinquantotto) trips up learners constantly — practice it deliberately
  rather than defaulting to the "expected" full form.
- Unlike English, Italian doesn't insert "and" between hundreds and the
  rest of the number: 322 is trecentoventidue, with no word for "and."

PRACTICE:
Say these numbers aloud: 21, 38, 47, 96, 100, 250, 1,984, 3,000,000.

LESSON 14: Days, Dates, and the Calendar
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers the days of the week, months, and how to state and ask
for dates — including a word-order difference from English that trips up
many learners.

GRAMMAR:
- Days of the week (not capitalized in Italian): lunedì (Monday), martedì
  (Tuesday), mercoledì (Wednesday), giovedì (Thursday), venerdì (Friday),
  sabato (Saturday), domenica (Sunday). Note domenica is the only one that
  doesn't end in the accented -ì.
- Months (also not capitalized): gennaio, febbraio, marzo, aprile, maggio,
  giugno, luglio, agosto, settembre, ottobre, novembre, dicembre.
- Useful time words: oggi (today), domani (tomorrow), dopodomani (day
  after tomorrow), ieri (yesterday), l'altro ieri (day before yesterday,
  literally "the other yesterday").
- To ask the date: Che giorno è oggi? or Quanti ne abbiamo oggi? (What's
  today's date?)
- To STATE a date, Italian reverses the English order: it's day + month
  (+ year), introduced by è + il + [number]:
    Oggi è il dieci febbraio duemilaventisei. (Today is February 10, 2026.)
  Compare to English "February tenth" — Italian says, essentially, "the
  tenth of February."
- The first of the month is the one exception that uses an ordinal instead
  of a cardinal number: il primo maggio (May 1st), but il due maggio, il
  tre maggio, etc. after that use plain cardinal numbers.
- To ask when something happens, use quando (when): Quando parti? (When
  are you leaving?)

VOCABULARY:
lunedì-domenica (loo-neh-DEE-doh-MEH-nee-kah) - Monday-Sunday
oggi (OHJ-jee) - today
domani (doh-MAH-nee) - tomorrow
ieri (YEH-ree) - yesterday
gennaio-dicembre (jehn-NAH-yoh-dee-CHEHM-breh) - January-December
il compleanno (eel kohm-pleh-AHN-noh) - the birthday
quando (KWAHN-doh) - when
la settimana (lah seht-tee-MAH-nah) - the week
il mese (eel MEH-zeh) - the month
l'anno (LAHN-noh) - the year

EXAMPLES:
Che giorno è oggi? Oggi è mercoledì. - keh JOHR-noh eh OHJ-jee? OHJ-jee eh
  mehr-koh-leh-DEE - What day is today? Today is Wednesday.
Il mio compleanno è il primo maggio. - eel MEE-oh kohm-pleh-AHN-noh eh eel
  PREE-moh MAHJ-joh - My birthday is May 1st.
Parto domani, il sedici marzo. - PAHR-toh doh-MAH-nee, eel SEH-dee-chee
  MAHR-tsoh - I'm leaving tomorrow, the sixteenth of March.
Il concerto è martedì sera. - eel kohn-CHEHR-toh eh mahr-teh-DEE SEH-rah -
  The concert is Tuesday evening.

NOTES:
- Remember the date-order flip: Italian never says "maggio primo," always
  "il primo maggio" — the number leads.
- Days and months stay lowercase in Italian even at the start of a
  sentence about them ("lunedì è il mio giorno preferito," not "Lunedì...
  " capitalized for grammatical reasons — though sentence-initial capitals
  still apply to the sentence itself, the word "lunedì" itself is never
  capitalized as a proper noun the way English does).

PRACTICE:
Write out today's full date in Italian, then answer: Che giorno è domani?
Quando è il tuo compleanno?

LESSON 15: Telling Time
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Telling time in Italian uses "it is" differently depending on whether
you're talking about one o'clock or any other hour, and everyday Italy
runs on the 24-hour clock for anything official. This lesson covers both
systems.

GRAMMAR:
- Two equivalent ways to ask the time: Che ora è? or Che ore sono? (both
  mean "What time is it?")
- For exactly 1:00, noon, or midnight, use the SINGULAR verb è:
    È l'una. (It's one o'clock.)
    È mezzogiorno. (It's noon.)
    È mezzanotte. (It's midnight.)
- For every other hour, use the PLURAL verb sono + le + the number (because
  you're literally saying "they are the [hours]"):
    Sono le due. (It's two o'clock.)
    Sono le sette. (It's seven o'clock.)
- Minutes past the hour are added with e (and): sono le tre e dieci (3:10);
  common fixed expressions: e un quarto (quarter past), e mezzo (half
  past), e tre quarti (quarter to the next hour, less common than the
  Italian convention of counting toward the next hour, e.g. le quattro
  meno un quarto for 3:45).
- To ask what time something starts, add the preposition a, which
  contracts with the article: A che ora inizia? (What time does it
  start?) All'una. (At one.) Alle dieci. (At ten.) A mezzogiorno. (At
  noon.)
- Italy uses the 24-hour clock for all official schedules — trains,
  planes, museum hours, showtimes. 13:00 = l'una del pomeriggio in casual
  speech, but written/spoken officially as le tredici; 20:00 = le venti.

VOCABULARY:
che ora è? (keh OH-rah eh) - what time is it?
è l'una (eh LOO-nah) - it's one o'clock
sono le... (SOH-noh leh) - it's ... o'clock
mezzogiorno (mehd-dzoh-JOHR-noh) - noon
mezzanotte (mehd-dzah-NOHT-teh) - midnight
un quarto (oon KWAHR-toh) - a quarter
mezzo/mezza (MEHD-dzoh/MEHD-dzah) - half
in punto (een POON-toh) - exactly, on the dot
di mattina (dee maht-TEE-nah) - in the morning (a.m.)
di sera (dee SEH-rah) - in the evening (p.m.)

EXAMPLES:
Che ore sono? Sono le quattro e mezzo. - keh OH-reh SOH-noh? SOH-noh leh
  KWAHT-troh eh MEHD-dzoh - What time is it? It's four-thirty.
Il treno parte alle diciassette e trenta. - eel TREH-noh PAHR-teh AHL-leh
  dee-chahs-SEHT-teh eh TREHN-tah - The train leaves at 17:30 (5:30 p.m.).
A che ora inizia il film? Alle nove di sera. - ah keh OH-rah ee-NEE-tsyah
  eel feelm? AHL-leh NOH-veh dee SEH-rah - What time does the movie start?
  At nine p.m.
È mezzanotte, devo andare a dormire. - eh mehd-dzah-NOHT-teh, DEH-voh
  ahn-DAH-reh ah dohr-MEE-reh - It's midnight, I have to go to sleep.

NOTES:
- The è vs. sono switch (singular for 1:00/noon/midnight, plural for
  everything else) is the single most common time-telling error for
  learners — pause and check which case you're in before speaking.
- Even though casual conversation often uses the 12-hour system with
  "di mattina/di pomeriggio/di sera" for clarity, be ready to recognize
  24-hour times on posted schedules (le venti = 8:00 p.m.).

PRACTICE:
Say these times aloud in Italian: 1:00, 6:15, 12:00 (noon), 9:45, and
20:30 using the 24-hour convention.

LESSON 16: Weather and the Seasons
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Talking about weather is universal small talk, and Italian leans heavily
on the verb fare (to do/make) for weather expressions, echoing the fare
idioms you'll expand on later. This lesson covers the four seasons and how
to describe conditions.

GRAMMAR:
- Many weather expressions use fare (to do/make), used impersonally in the
  third person: fa bel tempo (the weather is nice, lit. "it makes nice
  weather"), fa caldo (it's hot), fa freddo (it's cold), fa brutto tempo
  (the weather is bad).
- Some weather verbs stand alone (no fare needed): piove (it's raining,
  from piovere, to rain), nevica (it's snowing, from nevicare, to snow),
  tira vento (it's windy).
- To ask about the weather: Che tempo fa? (What's the weather like?) or
  Com'è il tempo oggi? (How's the weather today?)
- The four seasons: la primavera (spring), l'estate (summer), l'autunno
  (fall/autumn), l'inverno (winter). Use in + season (no article) to say
  "in [season]": in primavera, in estate, in autunno, in inverno — though
  d'estate and d'inverno are also common contracted forms.

VOCABULARY:
che tempo fa? (keh TEHM-poh fah) - what's the weather like?
fa caldo (fah KAHL-doh) - it's hot
fa freddo (fah FREHD-doh) - it's cold
fa bel tempo (fah behl TEHM-poh) - the weather is nice
piove (PYOH-veh) - it's raining
nevica (NEH-vee-kah) - it's snowing
la primavera (lah pree-mah-VEH-rah) - spring
l'estate (f.) (lehs-TAH-teh) - summer
l'autunno (lou-TOON-noh) - autumn, fall
l'inverno (leen-VEHR-noh) - winter

EXAMPLES:
Che tempo fa oggi? Fa molto caldo. - keh TEHM-poh fah OHJ-jee? fah MOHL-toh
  KAHL-doh - What's the weather like today? It's very hot.
D'inverno nevica spesso in montagna. - deen-VEHR-noh NEH-vee-kah SPEHS-soh
  een mohn-TAH-nyah - In winter it often snows in the mountains.
Piove, porta l'ombrello! - PYOH-veh, POHR-tah lohm-BREHL-loh - It's
  raining, bring an umbrella!
Le piace Milano? Sì, ma non il clima; fa molto freddo in inverno. - leh
  PYAH-cheh mee-LAH-noh? see, mah nohn eel KLEE-mah; fah MOHL-toh FREHD-doh
  een een-VEHR-noh - Do you like Milan? Yes, but not the climate; it's
  very cold in winter.

NOTES:
- Don't try to translate "it's raining" word-for-word with essere — piove
  is a standalone impersonal verb form, not "è pioggia."
- Weather + fare idioms extend the pattern of avere idioms from Lesson 9
  (fare una domanda, fare una passeggiata) — fare is a genuinely
  multi-purpose verb worth watching for throughout the course.

PRACTICE:
Describe today's weather in Italian, then describe typical weather for
each of the four seasons where you live.

LESSON 17: The Metric System
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italy uses the metric system exclusively, so a little vocabulary here goes
a long way for shopping, cooking, travel distances, and understanding
weather reports.

GRAMMAR:
- Length and distance use metri (meters) and chilometri (kilometers,
  abbreviated km); short lengths use centimetri (centimeters).
- Weight uses chili/chilogrammi (kilograms, abbreviated kg) and grammi
  (grams); un etto is a common everyday unit meaning 100 grams, especially
  at delis and markets (Lesson 24 covers food shopping in depth).
- Liquid volume uses litri (liters).
- Temperature is measured in gradi Celsius (degrees Celsius) — 0°C
  freezing, and body-comfortable room temperature is around 20-22°C, useful
  to know when reading an Italian weather forecast.
- The construction for measurements mirrors English fairly closely:
  Quanto pesa? (How much does it weigh?) Pesa due chili. (It weighs two
  kilos.) Quanto è lontano? (How far is it?) È a cinque chilometri. (It's
  five kilometers away.)

VOCABULARY:
il metro (eel MEH-troh) - the meter
il chilometro (eel kee-LOH-meh-troh) - the kilometer
il centimetro (eel chehn-TEE-meh-troh) - the centimeter
il chilo/chilogrammo (eel KEE-loh) - the kilogram
il grammo (eel GRAHM-moh) - the gram
un etto (oon EHT-toh) - 100 grams
il litro (eel LEE-troh) - the liter
grado (GRAH-doh) - degree
quanto pesa? (KWAHN-toh PEH-zah) - how much does it weigh?
quanto è lontano? (KWAHN-toh eh lohn-TAH-noh) - how far is it?

EXAMPLES:
Vorrei mezzo chilo di prosciutto. - vohr-RAY MEHD-dzoh KEE-loh dee
  proh-SHOOT-toh - I'd like half a kilo of prosciutto.
La città è a dieci chilometri da qui. - lah cheet-TAH eh ah DYEH-chee
  kee-LOH-meh-tree dah kwee - The city is ten kilometers from here.
Oggi ci sono trenta gradi. - OHJ-jee chee SOH-noh TREHN-tah GRAH-dee -
  Today it's thirty degrees (Celsius).
Un litro d'acqua, per favore. - oon LEE-troh DAHK-kwah, pehr fah-VOH-reh -
  A liter of water, please.

NOTES:
- If you're used to Fahrenheit, remember 30°C is a genuinely hot summer
  day, not a chilly one — roughly 86°F.
- "Un etto" (100 grams) is worth memorizing specifically, since it's the
  everyday unit Italians use at the deli counter and produce stand rather
  than always switching to grams or kilos.

PRACTICE:
Convert and say in Italian: your height in meters/centimeters, a
comfortable room temperature in Celsius, and the weight of a kilo of
apples.

LESSON 18: Home Sweet Home — Housing Vocabulary and Asking Directions
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson pairs two practical topics: describing where you live (rooms,
housing types, ordinal numbers for floors) and asking for directions with
the verbs of motion you'll need to get around.

GRAMMAR:
- Ordinal numbers (first, second, third...) are used constantly for
  building floors: primo, secondo, terzo, quarto, quinto, sesto, settimo,
  ottavo, nono, decimo. They behave like type-1 adjectives and agree with
  the noun: il primo piano (the first floor), la seconda porta (the second
  door).
  Note: in Italian buildings, il piano terra (ground floor) is separate
  from il primo piano (which is one floor UP from ground level, i.e. what
  Americans call the "second floor").
- Housing vocabulary: un appartamento (apartment), una casa (house), un
  monolocale (studio apartment), l'affitto (rent), il palazzo (building).
- To ask for directions, use dov'è...? (where is...?) for singular, dove
  sono...? for plural, or Come arrivo a...? (How do I get to...?)
- The main verbs of motion, all irregular, are essential here: andare (to
  go), venire (to come). Andare pairs with the preposition a for cities and
  in for most other places: vado a Roma (I'm going to Rome) but vado in
  centro (I'm going downtown), vado in banca (I'm going to the bank).
- Directional vocabulary: a destra (to the right), a sinistra (to the
  left), sempre dritto (straight ahead), all'angolo (at/on the corner),
  vicino a (near), lontano da (far from).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Pronoun     andare (to go)    venire (to come)
io          vado              vengo
tu          vai               vieni
lui/lei     va                viene
noi         andiamo           veniamo
voi         andate            venite
loro        vanno             vengono

VOCABULARY:
l'appartamento (lahp-pahr-tah-MEHN-toh) - the apartment
il piano (eel PYAH-noh) - the floor (of a building)
il piano terra (eel PYAH-noh TEHR-rah) - the ground floor
la camera (lah KAH-meh-rah) - the bedroom
la cucina (lah koo-CHEE-nah) - the kitchen
il bagno (eel BAH-nyoh) - the bathroom
dov'è...? (doh-VEH) - where is...?
a destra/sinistra (ah DEHS-trah/see-NEES-trah) - to the right/left
sempre dritto (SEHM-preh DREET-toh) - straight ahead
all'angolo (ahl-LAHN-goh-loh) - at the corner

EXAMPLES:
Abito in un appartamento al terzo piano. - AH-bee-toh een oon
  ahp-pahr-tah-MEHN-toh ahl TEHR-tsoh PYAH-noh - I live in an apartment on
  the third floor.
Scusi, dov'è la stazione? - SKOO-zee, doh-VEH lah stah-TSYOH-neh - Excuse
  me, where is the station?
Vada sempre dritto e poi giri a destra. - VAH-dah SEHM-preh DREET-toh eh
  poy JEE-ree ah DEHS-trah - Go straight ahead and then turn right.
La banca è vicino alla piazza, all'angolo. - lah BAHN-kah eh vee-CHEE-noh
  AHL-lah PYAHT-tsah, ahl-LAHN-goh-loh - The bank is near the square, on
  the corner.

NOTES:
- Remember the floor-numbering offset: what Americans call the "second
  floor" is il primo piano in Italy, since il piano terra is its own
  separate ground level, not "floor one."
- Andare's preposition depends on the destination type — cities take a,
  most enclosed or general places take in (in banca, in centro, in
  Italia), and this is worth memorizing case by case rather than by rule
  alone.

PRACTICE:
Describe your home (how many rooms, which floor) in three sentences, then
give directions from your front door to the nearest supermarket.
