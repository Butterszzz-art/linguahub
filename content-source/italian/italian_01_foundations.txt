LESSON 1: The Italian Alphabet and Vowel Sounds
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italian spelling is far more consistent than English spelling — once you
learn the rules in this lesson, you can pronounce almost any Italian word
correctly on sight, even one you've never seen before. This lesson covers
the alphabet and the five vowel sounds that are the backbone of Italian
pronunciation.

GRAMMAR:
- The Italian alphabet (l'alfabeto) has only 21 native letters. J, K, W, X,
  and Y exist only in foreign loanwords (jeans, whisky, taxi, yogurt).
- Every Italian vowel has exactly ONE pure sound — it never glides the way
  English vowels do (compare English "go" [gow-oo] to Italian "vado," where
  the o stays a clean, single "oh").
- The five vowel sounds:
    a  -> "ah" (as in "father")            casa (KAH-zah) (house)
    e  -> "eh" (as in "bet")               bene (BEH-neh) (well)
    i  -> "ee" (as in "machine")           vita (VEE-tah) (life)
    o  -> "oh" (as in "boat," no glide)    dove (DOH-veh) (where)
    u  -> "oo" (as in "moon")              tu (too) (you)
- Double vowels are both pronounced separately: mai (MAH-ee) (never).
- Word stress usually falls on the second-to-last syllable (the "penultimate"
  syllable): parLAre, amiCO. When a word carries a written accent (` or ´)
  on its final vowel, the stress falls there instead: città (cheet-TAH)
  (city), perché (pehr-KEH) (why/because).

VOCABULARY:
casa (KAH-zah) - house
bene (BEH-neh) - well, fine
vita (VEE-tah) - life
dove (DOH-veh) - where
tu (too) - you (informal)
città (cheet-TAH) - city
perché (pehr-KEH) - why, because
sole (SOH-leh) - sun
luna (LOO-nah) - moon
mare (MAH-reh) - sea

EXAMPLES:
Dove abiti? - DOH-veh AH-bee-tee - Where do you live?
Come stai? Bene, grazie. - KOH-meh STAH-ee? BEH-neh, GRAH-tsee-eh - How are
  you? Fine, thanks.
La città è grande. - lah cheet-TAH eh GRAHN-deh - The city is big.
Il sole e la luna - eel SOH-leh eh lah LOO-nah - The sun and the moon

NOTES:
- English speakers often want to add a glide to o and e ("oh-oo," "eh-ee").
  Resist it — hold the vowel steady from start to finish.
- If a word looks unfamiliar and has no written accent, guess stress on the
  second-to-last syllable first; it's right more often than not.

PRACTICE:
Read these words aloud, holding each vowel pure and steady: pane, vino,
amico, dopo, cultura. Then try città and università, remembering the
accent shifts the stress to the final syllable.

LESSON 2: Consonants and Consonant Combinations
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Most Italian consonants sound like their English counterparts, but a
handful change pronunciation depending on which vowel follows them. This
lesson gives you the two rules (for C and G) that unlock hundreds of words,
plus the special consonant clusters GN, GL, and SC.

GRAMMAR:
- C and G each have a "hard" and "soft" sound, and the vowel that follows
  decides which one you use:
    C + a, o, u, or a consonant -> hard "k" sound: casa (KAH-zah), colpa
      (KOHL-pah), cuore (KWOH-reh)
    C + e, i -> soft "ch" sound (as in "church"): cena (CHEH-nah), cibo
      (CHEE-boh)
    G + a, o, u, or a consonant -> hard "g" sound: gamba (GAHM-bah), gomma
      (GOHM-mah)
    G + e, i -> soft "j" sound (as in "jump"): gelato (jeh-LAH-toh),
      giorno (JOHR-noh)
- To get a HARD c or g sound before e or i, insert an h: che (keh) (what),
  spaghetti (spah-GEHT-tee), ghiaccio (GYAHCH-choh) (ice).
- To get a SOFT ch or j sound before a, o, or u, insert a silent i: ciao
  (chow), giacca (JAHK-kah) (jacket) — the i is not pronounced, it just
  signals the soft sound.
- Special clusters:
    GN -> like "ny" in "canyon": gnocchi (NYOHK-kee), bagno (BAH-nyoh)
      (bathroom)
    GL (before i) -> like "lli" in "million": famiglia (fah-MEE-lyah)
      (family), figlio (FEE-lyoh) (son)
    SC + e/i -> "sh": scena (SHEH-nah) (scene), pesce (PEH-sheh) (fish)
    SC + a/o/u/h -> "sk": scuola (SKWOH-lah) (school), scherzo (SKEHR-tsoh)
      (joke)
- Double consonants are held longer than single ones, and this genuinely
  changes meaning: nono (NOH-noh) (ninth) vs. nonno (NOHN-noh)
  (grandfather); capello (kah-PEHL-loh) (hair) vs. cappello (kahp-PEHL-loh)
  (hat).
- R is trilled/tapped at the front of the mouth, not pronounced in the
  throat like English or French r.
- H is always silent on its own — its only job is to switch c/g between
  hard and soft.

VOCABULARY:
gelato (jeh-LAH-toh) - ice cream
giorno (JOHR-noh) - day
famiglia (fah-MEE-lyah) - family
figlio/figlia (FEE-lyoh/FEE-lyah) - son/daughter
pesce (PEH-sheh) - fish
scuola (SKWOH-lah) - school
bagno (BAH-nyoh) - bathroom
ciao (chow) - hi, bye
spaghetti (spah-GEHT-tee) - spaghetti
cappello (kahp-PEHL-loh) - hat

EXAMPLES:
Buongiorno! Come va la famiglia? - bwohn-JOHR-noh! KOH-meh vah lah
  fah-MEE-lyah? - Good morning! How's the family?
Vado a scuola con mio figlio. - VAH-doh ah SKWOH-lah kohn MEE-oh FEE-lyoh -
  I'm going to school with my son.
Mangiamo il pesce stasera. - mahn-JAH-moh eel PEH-sheh stah-SEH-rah -
  We're eating fish tonight.

NOTES:
- The most common beginner slip is treating GLI like the English "gl" in
  "glass" — it should sound closer to the "lli" in "million." Practice
  famiglia and figlio slowly.
- Doubled consonants are not optional flourishes — capello/cappello and
  pena/penna (pen) are genuinely different words, so lean into the double
  sound rather than rushing past it.

PRACTICE:
Sort these words by whether their C or G is hard or soft, then say them
aloud: cena, cane, gente, gatto, ciao, gara, cibo, gonna.

================================================================
LESSON 3: You Already Know Some Italian — Cognates and Common Expressions
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Before diving into grammar, it helps to realize how much Italian you
already recognize. Italian and English share a huge number of cognates —
words with a common origin that look and often sound alike — plus a set of
everyday expressions that are useful from your very first conversation.

GRAMMAR:
- A cognate is a word that shares an origin with — and usually a visible
  resemblance to — a word in another language. Italian and English share
  thousands of them because English borrowed heavily from Latin (Italian's
  direct parent) via French.
- Watch for reliable patterns: English words ending in -tion often match
  Italian words ending in -zione (informazione = information; nazione =
  nation); English -ty often matches Italian -tà (università = university;
  città = city); English -ble often matches Italian -bile (incredibile =
  incredible; possibile = possible).
- Not every similar-looking word is a true cognate — some are "false
  friends" that look alike but mean different things (see Lesson 44 for a
  full list). For now, trust the patterns above; they're reliable.
- Popular, everyday expressions are worth memorizing as fixed chunks even
  before you understand every grammatical piece inside them.

VOCABULARY:
aeroporto (ah-eh-roh-POHR-toh) - airport
attenzione (aht-tehn-TSYOH-neh) - attention
importante (eem-pohr-TAHN-teh) - important
incredibile (een-kreh-DEE-bee-leh) - incredible
informazione (een-fohr-mah-TSYOH-neh) - information
università (oo-nee-vehr-see-TAH) - university
Che c'è? (keh cheh) - What's up?
D'accordo! (dahk-KOHR-doh) - Agreed! Deal!
Non c'è di che. (nohn cheh dee keh) - You're welcome.
Va bene! (vah BEH-neh) - Okay!

EXAMPLES:
L'aeroporto è molto importante per la città. - lah-eh-roh-POHR-toh eh
  MOHL-toh eem-pohr-TAHN-teh pehr lah cheet-TAH - The airport is very
  important for the city.
Ho bisogno di informazione. - oh bee-ZOH-nyoh dee een-fohr-mah-TSYOH-neh -
  I need information.
"Grazie mille!" "Non c'è di che." - GRAH-tsee-eh MEEL-leh! Nohn cheh dee
  keh - "Thanks a lot!" "You're welcome."

NOTES:
- Lean on cognates to read signs, menus, and simple text right away — your
  passive vocabulary is much bigger than your active one at this stage.
- Reserve full trust for false friends until Lesson 44; a few common
  Italian words (simpatico, educazione, sensibile) look like English words
  but carry different meanings.

PRACTICE:
Guess the English meaning of these Italian words before checking: nazione,
possibile, comunicazione, terribile, fantastico. Then use "D'accordo!" and
"Va bene!" in a short made-up exchange.

LESSON 4: Sentence Structure and Noun Gender
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italian sentences follow the same basic order as English — subject, verb,
object — which makes the biggest adjustment not word order but noun
gender, something English doesn't have. Every Italian noun is either
masculine or feminine, and that single fact ripples out into articles,
adjectives, and pronouns throughout the language.

GRAMMAR:
- Basic word order matches English: Marco parla italiano. (Marco speaks
  Italian.) Subject-Verb-Object.
- Every noun has a grammatical gender (masculine or feminine) and a number
  (singular or plural). Gender is not about biological sex for objects —
  it's a grammatical category you memorize along with the word.
- The most reliable pattern:
    Feminine nouns usually end in -a (singular) / -e (plural):
      una casa -> due case (a house -> two houses)
    Masculine nouns usually end in -o (singular) / -i (plural):
      un libro -> due libri (a book -> two books)
    Nouns ending in -e can be either gender, and end in -i in the plural
      either way: un esame (m.) -> due esami; una lezione (f.) -> due
      lezioni. You memorize the gender of -e nouns individually.
- Handy shortcut: almost any noun ending in -zione is feminine (nazione,
  lezione, informazione).
- Invariable nouns keep the same form in singular and plural. Three types:
    1. Nouns ending in an accented vowel: caffè -> caffè; città -> città
    2. Nouns ending in a consonant (rare, mostly borrowed words): bar ->
       bar; film -> film
    3. Abbreviated words: bici (bike) -> bici; foto -> foto; auto -> auto

VOCABULARY:
libro (m.) (LEE-broh) - book
lezione (f.) (leh-TSYOH-neh) - lesson, class
esame (m.) (eh-ZAH-meh) - exam
città (f.) (cheet-TAH) - city
caffè (m.) (kahf-FEH) - coffee
bici (f.) (BEE-chee) - bike
film (m.) (feelm) - movie
studente/studentessa (stoo-DEHN-teh/stoo-dehn-TEHS-sah) - student (m./f.)
amico/amica (ah-MEE-koh/ah-MEE-kah) - friend (m./f.)
ragazzo/ragazza (rah-GAHTS-tsoh/rah-GAHTS-tsah) - boy/girl

EXAMPLES:
Marco parla italiano. - MAHR-koh PAHR-lah ee-tah-LYAH-noh - Marco speaks
  Italian.
Ho un esame difficile. - oh oon eh-ZAH-meh deef-FEE-chee-leh - I have a
  difficult exam.
Le mie amiche studiano in città. - leh MEE-eh ah-MEE-keh STOO-dyah-noh een
  cheet-TAH - My friends (f.) study in the city.
Prendo un caffè e vado. - PREHN-doh oon kahf-FEH eh VAH-doh - I'll have a
  coffee and go.

NOTES:
- There is no reliable shortcut for -e nouns: gender must be learned word
  by word, ideally alongside the article (e.g., learn "il caffè," not just
  "caffè").
- Because articles and adjectives must agree with noun gender (Lessons 5
  and 6), getting gender right from the start saves a lot of correction
  later — always learn new nouns with their article attached.

PRACTICE:
Decide masculine or feminine, then give the plural: pizza, treno, notte,
professore, chiave, museo.

LESSON 5: Definite and Indefinite Articles
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italian articles ("a/an" and "the") agree with the noun in both gender and
number, and — unlike English — the choice also depends on the very first
sound of the noun that follows. This lesson lays out both systems clearly.

GRAMMAR:
- Indefinite articles ("a/an," always singular):
    una  -> feminine nouns starting with a consonant: una ragazza (a girl)
    un'  -> feminine nouns starting with a vowel: un'amica (a friend)
    un   -> masculine nouns starting with a vowel or most consonants: un
            amico, un ragazzo
    uno  -> masculine nouns starting with s+consonant (s impura), z, gn,
            ps, or pn: uno studente, uno zio (an uncle), uno psicologo
- Definite articles ("the") — these are the trickiest part of Italian
  grammar to memorize, so give this table real attention:
    Masculine singular: il (before most consonants) / lo (before s+cons.,
      z, gn, ps, pn) / l' (before a vowel)
    Masculine plural: i (before most consonants) / gli (before s+cons., z,
      gn, ps, pn, and before vowels)
    Feminine singular: la (before consonants) / l' (before vowels)
    Feminine plural: le (for all feminine plurals, regardless of the
      following sound)
- Examples showing the pattern: il libro/i libri; lo studente/gli
  studenti; l'amico/gli amici; la casa/le case; l'amica/le amiche.
- Italian uses the definite article far more often than English does —
  notably with general or abstract nouns and languages: L'amore è cieco.
  (Love is blind.) Mi piace l'italiano. (I like Italian.) — English drops
  the article in both cases; Italian keeps it.

VOCABULARY:
zio/zia (DZEE-oh/DZEE-ah) - uncle/aunt
studente/studentessa (stoo-DEHN-teh) - student
amico/amica (ah-MEE-koh/ah-MEE-kah) - friend
psicologo (psee-KOH-loh-goh) - psychologist
libro (LEE-broh) - book
casa (KAH-zah) - house
amore (m.) (ah-MOH-reh) - love
italiano (ee-tah-LYAH-noh) - Italian (language)
gli occhi (lyee OHK-kee) - the eyes
gli studenti (lyee stoo-DEHN-tee) - the students

EXAMPLES:
Ho un'amica a Roma e uno zio a Milano. - oh oon-ah-MEE-kah ah ROH-mah eh
  OO-noh DZEE-oh ah mee-LAH-noh - I have a friend in Rome and an uncle in
  Milan.
Gli studenti studiano l'italiano. - lyee stoo-DEHN-tee STOO-dyah-noh
  lee-tah-LYAH-noh - The students study Italian.
La casa è grande, le case sono piccole. - lah KAH-zah eh GRAHN-deh, leh
  KAH-zeh SOH-noh PEEK-koh-leh - The house is big, the houses are small.

NOTES:
- Learners often default to "il" for every masculine noun out of habit —
  train your ear specifically on "lo" and "gli" before s+consonant, z, and
  gn words (lo studente, gli zii, lo zaino).
- Remember the article-with-language rule: "Studio l'italiano," not
  "Studio italiano," when talking about the language in general.

PRACTICE:
Add the correct definite article: ___ zaino (backpack, m.), ___ amica (f.),
___ studente (m.), ___ case (f. pl.), ___ zii (uncles, m. pl.).

LESSON 6: Adjectives and Agreement
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italian adjectives change their ending to match the gender and number of
the noun they describe — a feature with no equivalent in English. This
lesson covers the two adjective patterns and where adjectives sit relative
to the noun.

GRAMMAR:
- There are two adjective types, based on how many distinct endings they
  have:
    Type 1 (four endings: -o, -a, -i, -e) — agrees in both gender and
      number, following the pattern of italiano:
        il ragazzo italiano (the Italian boy, m. sg.)
        i ragazzi italiani (the Italian boys, m. pl.)
        la ragazza italiana (the Italian girl, f. sg.)
        le ragazze italiane (the Italian girls, f. pl.)
      Other type-1 adjectives: alto/a (tall), piccolo/a (small), rosso/a
      (red).
    Type 2 (two endings: -e singular, -i plural) — agrees only in number,
      same form for masculine and feminine:
        l'esame facile / gli esami facili (the easy exam(s), m.)
        la prova facile / le prove facili (the easy test(s), f.)
      Other type-2 adjectives: grande (big), verde (green), francese
      (French), intelligente (intelligent).
- Position: most adjectives FOLLOW the noun (una casa grande, un libro
  interessante) — the opposite default from English. A short list of very
  common adjectives can go BEFORE the noun instead, especially bello
  (beautiful), buono (good), cattivo (bad), grande, and piccolo — placement
  can even shift the nuance slightly (un grand'uomo, "a great man," vs. un
  uomo grande, "a big/tall man").
- Adjectives of nationality always follow the noun and are not capitalized
  in Italian: la cucina italiana (Italian cuisine), un film francese (a
  French film).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(adjective agreement, not verb conjugation)
                     Masc. sg.   Masc. pl.   Fem. sg.    Fem. pl.
Type 1 (italiano)    italiano    italiani    italiana    italiane
Type 2 (grande)      grande      grandi      grande      grandi

VOCABULARY:
alto/a (AHL-toh/AHL-tah) - tall
piccolo/a (PEEK-koh-loh) - small
rosso/a (ROHS-soh) - red
grande (GRAHN-deh) - big, great
verde (VEHR-deh) - green
intelligente (een-tehl-lee-JEHN-teh) - intelligent
francese (frahn-CHEH-zeh) - French
bello/a (BEHL-loh) - beautiful
buono/a (BWOH-noh) - good
interessante (een-teh-rehs-SAHN-teh) - interesting

EXAMPLES:

  PEEK-koh-lah - I have a small red car.
Sono ragazze italiane e intelligenti. - SOH-noh rah-GAHTS-tseh
  ee-tah-LYAH-neh eh een-tehl-lee-JEHN-tee - They're smart Italian girls.
È un libro molto interessante. - eh oon LEE-broh MOHL-toh
  een-teh-rehs-SAHN-teh - It's a very interesting book.

NOTES:
- The most frequent beginner error is leaving an adjective in its "default"
  -o form regardless of the noun's gender — always check the noun first,
  then match the adjective to it.
- Don't force every adjective before the noun the way English does; when in
  doubt, put it after. You'll absorb the short "goes-before" list (bello,
  buono, grande, piccolo, cattivo, giovane, vecchio) with exposure.

PRACTICE:
Make each adjective agree with the noun: una casa (piccolo), due ragazzi
(francese), gli esami (difficile), la macchina (rosso).

================================================================
LESSON 7: Subject Pronouns and Formal vs. Informal "You"
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italian verb endings already show who's doing the action, so subject
pronouns (I, you, he, she...) are often dropped entirely — used only for
emphasis or clarity. This lesson also covers one of Italian's biggest
cultural adjustments for English speakers: choosing between the informal
and formal "you."

GRAMMAR:
- Subject pronouns:
    io (I) - tu (you, informal) - lui (he) - lei (she) - noi (we) -
    voi (you, plural) - loro (they)
- Because the verb ending already signals the subject (parlo = "I speak,"
  necessarily io), Italians normally drop the pronoun. Use it only:
    For contrast: Tu parli italiano, ma io parlo spagnolo. (You speak
      Italian, but I speak Spanish.)
    For emphasis: Vieni anche tu? (Are YOU coming too?)
    Standing alone: "Chi è?" "Sono io." ("Who is it?" "It's me.")
- Formal vs. informal "you": Italian has two ways to say "you" to one
  person, and choosing correctly matters socially.
    tu -> informal; use with friends, family, children, peers, anyone your
      own age you're on casual terms with.
    Lei -> formal (capitalized when written to show respect); use with
      strangers, superiors, shopkeepers, waitstaff, teachers, and anyone
      you don't know well. Grammatically, Lei behaves like "she" — it
      takes third-person singular verb forms even when addressing a man.
- The plural "you" (voi) covers a group regardless of formality in
  everyday speech.
- Custom dictates that the older or higher-status person is the one who
  invites a switch from Lei to tu ("Diamoci del tu" — "let's use tu with
  each other").

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Pronoun    Meaning              Verb form example (essere/to be)
io         I                    sono
tu         you (informal)       sei
lui/lei    he/she               è
Lei        you (formal)         è
noi        we                   siamo
voi        you (plural)         siete
loro       they                 sono

VOCABULARY:

Lei (ley) - you (formal)
voi (voi) - you (plural)
come stai? (KOH-meh STAH-ee) - how are you? (informal)

anche (AHN-keh) - also, too

EXAMPLES:
Ciao, come stai? - chow, KOH-meh STAH-ee - Hi, how are you? (informal)
Buongiorno, signora, come sta? - bwohn-JOHR-noh, see-NYOH-rah, KOH-meh
  stah - Good morning, ma'am, how are you? (formal)
Tu tifi per il Milan, ma io per la Juventus. - too TEE-fee pehr eel
  mee-LAHN, mah EE-oh pehr lah yoo-VEHN-toos - You root for Milan, but I
  root for Juventus.
Vieni anche tu alla festa? - VYEH-nee AHN-keh too AHL-lah FEHS-tah - Are
  you coming to the party too?

NOTES:
- Beginners often over-use subject pronouns out of habit from English —
  practice dropping io, tu, noi, etc. and letting the verb ending carry
  the meaning; it will sound much more natural.
- When unsure whether to use tu or Lei with someone new, default to Lei —
  it's the safer, more respectful choice, and Italians rarely take offense
  at being addressed too formally.

PRACTICE:
Choose tu or Lei for each situation: ordering coffee from a barista you've
never met; asking your little cousin a question; greeting your professor;
chatting with your best friend.

LESSON 8: Present Tense — Regular -are, -ere, and -ire Verbs
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Italian verbs fall into three families based on their infinitive ending:
-are, -ere, and -ire. Master the pattern for one verb in each family and
you can conjugate hundreds of regular verbs the same way.

GRAMMAR:
- To conjugate a regular verb, drop the infinitive ending (-are, -ere, or
  -ire) and add the ending that matches the subject.
- The present tense in Italian covers what English expresses with three
  different constructions: parlo can mean "I speak," "I do speak," or "I
  am speaking," depending on context.
- -ARE verbs (like parlare, to speak): io parlo, tu parli, lui/lei parla,
  noi parliamo, voi parlate, loro parlano
- -ERE verbs (like vivere, to live): io vivo, tu vivi, lui/lei vive, noi
  viviamo, voi vivete, loro vivono
- -IRE verbs, group 1 (like partire, to leave): io parto, tu parti, lui/lei
  parte, noi partiamo, voi partite, loro partono
- -IRE verbs, group 2 — "isc" verbs (like capire, to understand): these
  insert -isc- between the stem and ending in every form except noi and
  voi: io capisco, tu capisci, lui/lei capisce, noi capiamo, voi capite,
  loro capiscono. Other common isc verbs: finire (to finish), preferire
  (to prefer), pulire (to clean). There's no visual way to predict which
  -ire verbs take -isc-; you learn it verb by verb.

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Pronoun     parlare (-are)   vivere (-ere)   partire (-ire)   capire (-isc-)
io          parlo            vivo            parto            capisco
tu          parli            vivi             parti           capisci
lui/lei     parla            vive             parte           capisce
noi         parliamo         viviamo          partiamo         capiamo
voi         parlate          vivete           partite          capite
loro        parlano          vivono           partono          capiscono

VOCABULARY:

vivere (VEE-veh-reh) - to live
prendere (PREHN-deh-reh) - to take, to have (food/drink)
ripetere (ree-PEH-teh-reh) - to repeat
partire (pahr-TEE-reh) - to leave, depart
aprire (ah-PREE-reh) - to open
capire (kah-PEE-reh) - to understand
finire (fee-NEE-reh) - to finish

EXAMPLES:
Parlo un po' d'italiano. - PAHR-loh oon poh dee-tah-LYAH-noh - I speak a
  little Italian.
Studiamo insieme ogni sera. - stoo-DYAH-moh een-SYEH-meh OH-nyee SEH-rah -
  We study together every evening.
Non capisco bene, ma capisco di più ogni giorno. - nohn kah-PEES-koh BEH-
  neh, mah kah-PEES-koh dee pyoo OH-nyee JOHR-noh - I don't understand well,
  but I understand more every day.
A che ora parte il treno? - ah keh OH-rah PAHR-teh eel TREH-noh - What time
  does the train leave?

NOTES:
- There's no way to tell from the infinitive alone whether an -ire verb
  needs -isc-; when learning a new -ire verb, note its type immediately.
- Because subject pronouns are usually dropped (Lesson 7), the verb ending
  is doing double duty: it tells you both the action and the subject, so
  getting endings right matters even more in Italian than in English.

PRACTICE:
Conjugate mangiare and preferire (an isc verb) fully in the present tense,
then use each in one original sentence.

================================================================
LESSON 9: Essere and Avere, Avere Idioms, and the Modal Verbs
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Essere (to be) and avere (to have) are the two most important irregular
verbs in Italian — they're used constantly on their own and as helping
verbs for compound tenses later on. Avere also drives a set of idiomatic
expressions that don't translate word-for-word from English. This lesson
closes out Part 1 with these verbs plus the three modal verbs: dovere,
volere, and potere.

GRAMMAR:
- Essere (to be) and avere (to have) are both irregular and must be
  memorized outright — you'll use them in nearly every sentence.
- Idiomatic uses of avere: Italian expresses many states that English
  handles with "to be" using avere ("to have") instead — literally "to
  have hunger," "to have heat," and so on.
- The three modal (helping) verbs — dovere (must/have to), volere (to
  want), potere (can/may) — are conjugated themselves, and are followed
  by another verb in the INFINITIVE (unconjugated) form:
    Devo studiare. (I have to study.)
    Voglio dormire! (I want to sleep!)
    Posso entrare? (May I come in?)

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Pronoun     essere (to be)   avere (to have)   dovere      volere     potere
io          sono             ho                devo        voglio     posso
tu          sei              hai               devi        vuoi       puoi
lui/lei     è                ha                deve        vuole      può
noi         siamo            abbiamo           dobbiamo    vogliamo   possiamo
voi         siete            avete             dovete      volete     potete
loro        sono             hanno             devono      vogliono   possono

VOCABULARY:
avere fame (ah-VEH-reh FAH-meh) - to be hungry (lit. to have hunger)
avere sete (ah-VEH-reh SEH-teh) - to be thirsty
avere caldo (ah-VEH-reh KAHL-doh) - to be hot
avere freddo (ah-VEH-reh FREHD-doh) - to be cold
avere sonno (ah-VEH-reh SOHN-noh) - to be sleepy
avere ragione (ah-VEH-reh rah-JOH-neh) - to be right
avere torto (ah-VEH-reh TOHR-toh) - to be wrong
avere ... anni (ah-VEH-reh AHN-nee) - to be ... years old
avere bisogno di (ah-VEH-reh bee-ZOH-nyoh dee) - to need
avere voglia di (ah-VEH-reh VOH-lyah dee) - to feel like, crave

EXAMPLES:
Ho fame e ho sete! - oh FAH-meh eh oh SEH-teh - I'm hungry and thirsty!
Quanti anni hai? Ho ventotto anni. - KWAHN-tee AHN-nee AH-ee? oh
  vehn-TOHT-toh AHN-nee - How old are you? I'm twenty-eight.
Devo lavorare, ma voglio uscire. - DEH-voh lah-voh-RAH-reh, mah VOH-lyoh
  oo-SHEE-reh - I have to work, but I want to go out.
Non posso venire stasera. - nohn POHS-soh veh-NEE-reh stah-SEH-rah - I
  can't come tonight.

NOTES:
- English learners instinctively reach for "essere" to translate "to be
  hungry/cold/right," etc. — catch yourself and use avere instead for this
  whole family of expressions.
- After dovere, volere, or potere, the second verb always stays in the
  infinitive — never conjugate both verbs (devo studiare, not devo studio).

PRACTICE:
Translate using avere idioms: "I'm thirty years old." "We're cold." "She's
right." "Do you (informal) need anything?" Then write one sentence each
with dovere + infinitive and volere + infinitive.
