LESSON 37: Demonstratives and Possessives
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers two families of words that point to and claim
ownership of things: demonstratives (this/that) and possessives (my/your/
his/her). Both agree with the noun they modify, following patterns you've
already seen with adjectives.

GRAMMAR:
- Demonstrative adjectives/pronouns: questo/a/i/e (this/these) behaves
  like a regular Type 1 adjective (Lesson 6): questo libro, questa casa,
  questi libri, queste case.
- Quello (that/those) is trickier — it borrows endings from the definite
  article system (Lesson 5) depending on the noun that follows: quel
  libro (like "il"), quello studente (like "lo"), quell'amico (like
  "l'"), quella casa (like "la"), quei libri (like "i"), quegli studenti
  (like "gli"), quelle case (like "le"). Used alone as a pronoun ("that
  one"), it simplifies to quello/a/i/e.
- Possessive adjectives (my, your, his/her, our, your-pl, their) agree
  with the THING POSSESSED, not with the possessor — this is a genuine
  difference from English, where "his" and "her" depend on the owner's
  gender:
    il mio libro (my book) / la mia casa (my house) — same possessor,
    different endings based on what's owned
    Full set (masc. sg. shown): il mio, il tuo, il suo, il nostro, il
    vostro, il loro (their — invariable across gender/number, unlike the
    others)
- Possessives normally keep the definite article (il mio libro), EXCEPT
  with singular, unmodified family members, where the article drops: mia
  madre (my mother), tuo padre (your father), sua sorella (his/her
  sister) — but the article returns if the noun is plural or modified: i
  miei genitori (my parents), la mia sorella maggiore (my older sister).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(possessive adjectives, masculine singular form shown as the base)
Possessor      Masc. sg.   Fem. sg.    Masc. pl.   Fem. pl.
my             il mio      la mia      i miei      le mie
your (inf.)    il tuo      la tua      i tuoi      le tue
his/her/your(f) il suo     la sua      i suoi      le sue
our            il nostro   la nostra   i nostri    le nostre
your (pl.)     il vostro   la vostra   i vostri    le vostre
their          il loro     la loro     i loro      le loro

VOCABULARY:
questo/a (KWEHS-toh) - this
quello/a (KWEHL-loh) - that
il mio/la mia (eel MEE-oh/lah MEE-ah) - my/mine
il tuo/la tua (eel TOO-oh/lah TOO-ah) - your/yours (informal)
il suo/la sua (eel SOO-oh/lah SOO-ah) - his/her/its/your (formal)
il nostro/la nostra (eel NOHS-troh) - our/ours
il loro/la loro (eel LOH-roh) - their/theirs
la madre (lah MAH-dreh) - mother
il padre (eel PAH-dreh) - father
i genitori (ee jeh-nee-TOH-ree) - parents

EXAMPLES:
Questo libro è mio, quello è tuo. - KWEHS-toh LEE-broh eh MEE-oh, KWEHL-
  loh eh TOO-oh - This book is mine, that one is yours.
Mia madre e mio padre abitano a Torino. - MEE-ah MAH-dreh eh MEE-oh
  PAH-dreh AH-bee-tah-noh ah toh-REE-noh - My mother and father live in
  Turin.
I miei genitori sono in vacanza. - ee MYEH-ee jeh-nee-TOH-ree SOH-noh een
  vah-KAHN-tsah - My parents are on vacation.
Quella macchina rossa è la nostra. - KWEHL-lah MAHK-kee-nah ROHS-sah eh
  lah NOHS-trah - That red car is ours.

NOTES:
- The dropped article with singular family terms is one of the most
  distinctive features of Italian possessives — mia madre, not "la mia
  madre" — and it's worth practicing as a fixed exception.
- When quello functions as an adjective before a noun, it changes form
  like the definite article does; when it stands alone as a pronoun
  ("that one"), it simplifies back to the plain quello/a/i/e pattern.

PRACTICE:
Fill in the possessive: ___ (my) fratello, ___ (his) macchina, ___ (our)
casa, ___ (their) amici. Then use quello correctly before: studente,
amica, ragazzi, case.

LESSON 38: Handling Medical Emergencies
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Nobody wants to need this vocabulary, but knowing it in advance removes a
lot of stress if you ever do. This lesson covers describing symptoms,
body parts, and getting help at a doctor's office or pharmacy.

GRAMMAR:
- Basic body part vocabulary uses the regular gender patterns from
  Lesson 4, but several very common body parts have IRREGULAR plurals
  worth memorizing directly: il braccio -> le braccia (arms), il dito ->
  le dita (fingers), il ginocchio -> le ginocchia (knees), l'orecchio ->
  le orecchie (ears), la mano -> le mani (hands, irregular because mano
  is feminine despite its -o ending).
- To say something hurts, Italian has two common constructions:
    fare male (to hurt) + indirect pronoun: Mi fa male la gamba. (My leg
      hurts.) Use fa for one thing hurting, fanno for multiple: Mi fanno
      male gli occhi. (My eyes hurt.)
    avere mal di + [body part, no article]: Ho mal di testa. (I have a
      headache.) Ho mal di stomaco. (I have a stomachache.)
- To say you feel sick or unwell, use the reflexive verb sentirsi
  (Lesson 31): Mi sento male. (I feel sick.) Non mi sento bene. (I don't
  feel well.)
- Asking for help: Ho bisogno di un medico. (I need a doctor.) Dov'è il
  pronto soccorso? (Where's the emergency room?) Chiamate un'ambulanza!
  (Call an ambulance! — voi imperative, Lesson 36, since you may be
  addressing anyone nearby)

VOCABULARY:
la testa (lah TEHS-tah) - the head
la gamba (lah GAHM-bah) - the leg
lo stomaco (loh STOH-mah-koh) - the stomach
il braccio/le braccia (eel BRAHCH-choh/leh BRAHCH-chah) - arm(s)
fare male (FAH-reh MAH-leh) - to hurt
avere mal di... (ah-VEH-reh mahl dee) - to have a(n) ...ache
sentirsi male (sehn-TEER-see MAH-leh) - to feel sick
il pronto soccorso (eel PROHN-toh sohk-KOHR-soh) - emergency room
la farmacia (lah fahr-mah-CHEE-ah) - the pharmacy
la ricetta (lah ree-CHEHT-tah) - the prescription

EXAMPLES:
Mi fa male la gamba da ieri. - mee fah MAH-leh lah GAHM-bah dah YEH-ree -
  My leg has hurt since yesterday.
Ho mal di testa e mal di stomaco. - oh mahl dee TEHS-tah eh mahl dee
  STOH-mah-koh - I have a headache and a stomachache.
Non mi sento bene, ho bisogno di un medico. - nohn mee SEHN-toh BEH-neh,
  oh bee-ZOH-nyoh dee oon MEH-dee-koh - I don't feel well, I need a
  doctor.
Chiamate un'ambulanza, per favore! - kyah-MAH-teh oo-nahm-boo-LAHN-tsah,
  pehr fah-VOH-reh - Call an ambulance, please!

NOTES:
- The irregular plurals for body parts (braccia, dita, ginocchia,
  orecchie) are genuinely exceptions to normal noun rules — they were
  originally neuter nouns in Latin, which is why they behave differently;
  simply memorize them as a set.
- Fare male vs. avere mal di both work for describing pain, but avere mal
  di is generally reserved for a fixed set of common ailments (testa,
  stomaco, schiena, denti, gola), while fare male is more flexible and
  can describe any body part.

PRACTICE:
Describe three different symptoms using both constructions (fare male and
avere mal di), then write a short sentence asking for a doctor who speaks
English.

LESSON 39: Police, Accidents, and Lost Documents
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers a different kind of emergency vocabulary: reporting an
accident, dealing with the police, and handling a lost or stolen passport
or other documents.

GRAMMAR:
- To report that something has happened, use the passato prossimo
  (Lessons 26-27): C'è stato un incidente. (There's been an accident.)
  Note stato is the past participle of essere itself, used here
  impersonally.
- Asking and describing injury: Ci sono feriti? (Is anyone injured?) C'è
  una persona ferita. (There's an injured person.)
- To report something stolen or lost, mi hanno rubato (they stole from
  me / [thing] was stolen from me) is a common indirect construction: Mi
  hanno rubato il passaporto. (My passport was stolen. — lit. "They
  stole my passport from me.") For something simply lost (not stolen):
  Ho perso il passaporto. (I lost my passport.)
- Italy's emergency police number is 113, valid nationwide, and reachable
  for police assistance as well as ambulance dispatch in an emergency.
- Useful phrases for dealing with authorities: Ho bisogno di un
  avvocato. (I need a lawyer.) C'è un interprete che parla inglese? (Is
  there an interpreter who speaks English?)

VOCABULARY:
l'incidente (leen-chee-DEHN-teh) - the accident
ferito/a (feh-REE-toh) - injured
la polizia (lah poh-lee-TSEE-ah) - the police
rubare (roo-BAH-reh) - to steal
mi hanno rubato... (mee AHN-noh roo-BAH-toh) - ... was stolen from me
perdere (PEHR-deh-reh) - to lose
il passaporto (eel pahs-sah-POHR-toh) - the passport
il portafoglio (eel pohr-tah-FOH-lyoh) - the wallet
l'avvocato (lahv-voh-KAH-toh) - the lawyer
denunciare (deh-noon-CHAH-reh) - to report (to police)

EXAMPLES:
C'è stato un incidente in Piazza Mattei. - cheh STAH-toh oon
  een-chee-DEHN-teh een PYAHT-tsah maht-TEH-ee - There's been an accident
  in Piazza Mattei.
Mi hanno rubato il portafoglio sull'autobus. - mee AHN-noh roo-BAH-toh eel
  pohr-tah-FOH-lyoh sool-LOU-toh-boos - My wallet was stolen on the bus.
Devo denunciare il furto alla polizia. - DEH-voh deh-noon-CHAH-reh eel
  FOOR-toh AHL-lah poh-lee-TSEE-ah - I need to report the theft to the
  police.
Ho perso il passaporto, cosa devo fare? - oh PEHR-soh eel pahs-sah-
  POHR-toh, KOH-zah DEH-voh FAH-reh - I've lost my passport, what should
  I do?

NOTES:
- Keep "ho perso" (I lost) and "mi hanno rubato" (it was stolen from me)
  distinct — they have real practical consequences (insurance, police
  reports), so don't use them interchangeably.
- If you lose or have a passport stolen, contacting your country's
  embassy or consulate is the standard next step after reporting to local
  police — worth knowing even though it's outside the language lesson
  itself.

PRACTICE:
Write a short police report dialogue: describe what happened (an
accident or theft), whether anyone was hurt, and what was taken or lost.

LESSON 40: Interrogative Pronouns — Asking Questions Well
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
You've been using individual question words throughout this course (dove,
quando, come); this lesson pulls them together systematically and adds a
few more, including the tricky che vs. quale distinction.

GRAMMAR:
- Core interrogative words: chi? (who?), che cosa?/cosa?/che? (what? —
  all three are interchangeable, che cosa being the fullest form), dove?
  (where?), quando? (when?), come? (how?), perché? (why?), quanto/a/i/e?
  (how much/many? — agrees with the noun like an adjective), quale/i?
  (which?).
- Che vs. quale: che (or che cosa) asks an open-ended "what," with no
  implied set of options: Che cosa fai? (What are you doing?) Quale asks
  "which one" from an implied or stated set of choices, and agrees in
  number: Quale vuoi, questo o quello? (Which do you want, this one or
  that one?) Quali scarpe preferisci? (Which shoes do you prefer?)
- Quanto agrees fully like a Type 1 adjective when it modifies a noun:
  Quanto tempo? (How much time?) Quanti anni? (How many years?) Quante
  persone? (How many people?)
- Questions in Italian often don't need a special word-order inversion
  the way English does — rising intonation alone can turn a statement
  into a yes/no question: Parli italiano? (Do you speak Italian?) is
  identical in word order to the statement "Parli italiano" (You speak
  Italian), distinguished only by intonation (or the question mark in
  writing).

VOCABULARY:
chi? (kee) - who?
che cosa?/cosa? (keh KOH-zah) - what?
dove? (DOH-veh) - where?
quando? (KWAHN-doh) - when?
come? (KOH-meh) - how?
perché? (pehr-KEH) - why? / because
quanto/a/i/e? (KWAHN-toh) - how much/many?
quale/i? (KWAH-leh) - which?
di chi è? (dee kee eh) - whose is it?
com'è? (koh-MEH) - what's it like?

EXAMPLES:
Chi ha chiamato? Non lo so. - kee ah kyah-MAH-toh? nohn loh soh - Who
  called? I don't know.
Quale vestito preferisci, quello rosso o quello blu? - KWAH-leh
  vehs-TEE-toh preh-feh-REE-shee, KWEHL-loh ROHS-soh oh KWEHL-loh bloo -
  Which dress do you prefer, the red one or the blue one?
Quante persone vengono alla festa? - KWAHN-teh pehr-SOH-neh VEHN-goh-noh
  AHL-lah FEHS-tah - How many people are coming to the party?
Perché non vieni con noi? - pehr-KEH nohn VYEH-nee kohn noy - Why aren't
  you coming with us?

NOTES:
- If you're tempted to use "che" when there's an implied set of specific
  options (a menu, a list of colors, a choice between two things), reach
  for quale instead — this is the most common che/quale mix-up for
  learners.
- Written Italian marks questions with a question mark just like English,
  but spoken Italian relies heavily on intonation alone for yes/no
  questions — no auxiliary verb inversion is needed the way English uses
  "do/does."

PRACTICE:
Write five questions, one for each of: chi, quale, quanto, perché, and
come, on any topic you like.

LESSON 41: Family Vocabulary and Talking About Loved Ones
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson expands the family vocabulary introduced with madre/padre in
Lesson 37 into a full family tree, and practices possessives (Lesson 37)
and adjectives (Lesson 6) together to describe relatives in detail.

GRAMMAR:
- Full family vocabulary follows regular gender patterns (Lesson 4): most
  male relatives end in -o, most female relatives end in -a, with a
  handful of invariable or irregular forms (il/la nipote covers both
  "nephew/niece" and "grandson/granddaughter," distinguished only by
  context or article).
- Review: singular, unmodified family terms drop the article with
  possessives (Lesson 37): mio fratello, tua sorella, sua zia — but the
  article returns for plurals or when the noun is modified by an
  adjective: i miei fratelli, la mia sorella minore (my younger sister).
- To describe family relationships and resemblances, common constructions
  include: assomigliare a (to resemble): Assomiglio a mio padre. (I
  resemble my father.) and avere ... anni (Lesson 9) for ages: Mio
  fratello ha ventidue anni. (My brother is twenty-two.)
- Marital status vocabulary: sposato/a (married), single (invariable,
  borrowed from English), divorziato/a (divorced), fidanzato/a (engaged/
  boyfriend-girlfriend).

VOCABULARY:
il fratello/la sorella (eel frah-TEHL-loh/lah soh-REHL-lah) - brother/
  sister
il figlio/la figlia (eel FEE-lyoh/lah FEE-lyah) - son/daughter
il nonno/la nonna (eel NOHN-noh/lah NOHN-nah) - grandfather/grandmother
lo zio/la zia (loh DZEE-oh/lah DZEE-ah) - uncle/aunt
il cugino/la cugina (eel koo-JEE-noh/lah koo-JEE-nah) - cousin
il marito/la moglie (eel mah-REE-toh/lah MOH-lyeh) - husband/wife
sposato/a (spoh-ZAH-toh) - married
assomigliare a (ahs-soh-mee-LYAH-reh ah) - to resemble
maggiore/minore (mahj-JOH-reh/mee-NOH-reh) - older/younger
unico/a (OO-nee-koh) - only (as in only child)

EXAMPLES:
Ho due fratelli e una sorella minore. - oh DOO-eh frah-TEHL-lee eh OO-
  nah soh-REHL-lah mee-NOH-reh - I have two brothers and a younger
  sister.
I miei nonni abitano ancora in Toscana. - ee MYEH-ee NOHN-nee AH-bee-
  tah-noh ahn-KOH-rah een tohs-KAH-nah - My grandparents still live in
  Tuscany.
Assomiglio molto a mia madre. - ahs-soh-MEE-lyoh MOHL-toh ah MEE-ah
  MAH-dreh - I look a lot like my mother.
Mio cugino si è sposato l'anno scorso. - MEE-oh koo-JEE-noh see eh
  spoh-ZAH-toh LAHN-noh SKOHR-soh - My cousin got married last year.

NOTES:
- Il/la nipote covers three different English relationships (nephew,
  niece, grandchild) — context or an added clarifying phrase (nipote di
  mio fratello, "my brother's nephew/niece") resolves the ambiguity when
  it matters.
- Combine this lesson with Lesson 6's adjectives freely — describing
  family members (mia sorella maggiore è molto simpatica) is some of the
  most natural practice ground for agreement rules.

PRACTICE:
Describe your immediate family (at least four relatives) including one
age, one resemblance, and one adjective describing personality for each
person.

LESSON 42: Small Talk Wrap-Up — Reflexive Verbs in Everyday Conversation
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson closes the loop on reflexive verbs (Lesson 31) with additional
everyday expressions, and reviews the full toolkit of small talk from
throughout the course — a natural bridge into real conversation practice.

GRAMMAR:
- Additional common reflexive verbs worth adding to your toolkit:
  ricordarsi (to remember), dimenticarsi (to forget), preoccuparsi (to
  worry), arrabbiarsi (to get angry), annoiarsi (to get bored),
  innamorarsi (to fall in love), trovarsi (to find oneself, to get along
  — Mi trovo bene qui, "I'm comfortable/happy here").
- Reflexive verbs can also express reciprocal actions between two or more
  people (each other): Ci vediamo domani. (We'll see each other tomorrow
  / See you tomorrow.) Si conoscono da anni. (They've known each other
  for years.) The pronoun and structure are identical to true reflexives
  — context tells you which meaning applies.
- Reviewing the small-talk toolkit built across this course: greetings
  and formality (Lessons 7, 10), introductions (11), origins and language
  (12), talking about work (29), weather (16), and now describing family
  (41) — combine these freely; real conversations rarely stay inside one
  lesson's topic.

VOCABULARY:
ricordarsi (ree-kohr-DAHR-see) - to remember
dimenticarsi (dee-mehn-tee-KAHR-see) - to forget
preoccuparsi (preh-ohk-koo-PAHR-see) - to worry
arrabbiarsi (ahr-rahb-BYAHR-see) - to get angry
annoiarsi (ahn-noh-YAHR-see) - to get bored
innamorarsi (een-nah-moh-RAHR-see) - to fall in love
trovarsi bene (troh-VAHR-see BEH-neh) - to feel comfortable, get along
ci vediamo (chee veh-DYAH-moh) - see you, we'll see each other
non ti preoccupare (nohn tee preh-ohk-koo-PAH-reh) - don't worry (inf.)
si conoscono (see koh-NOH-skoh-noh) - they know each other

EXAMPLES:
Non ti preoccupare, andrà tutto bene. - nohn tee preh-ohk-koo-PAH-reh,
  ahn-DRAH TOOT-toh BEH-neh - Don't worry, everything will be fine.
Mi sono innamorato di questa città. - mee SOH-noh een-nah-moh-RAH-toh
  dee KWEHS-tah cheet-TAH - I've fallen in love with this city.
Ci vediamo sabato, va bene? - chee veh-DYAH-moh SAH-bah-toh, vah
  BEH-neh - See you Saturday, okay?
Mi trovo davvero bene in Italia. - mee TROH-voh dahv-VEH-roh BEH-neh
  een ee-TAH-lyah - I really feel at home in Italy.

NOTES:
- Watch for the reciprocal use of reflexive pronouns (ci vediamo, si
  conoscono) — the grammar looks identical to a true reflexive, but the
  meaning shifts to "each other" whenever the subject is plural and the
  action logically involves more than one person acting on each other.
- This is a good point to go back through Parts 1-4 and combine
  vocabulary across lessons in free conversation practice — real fluency
  comes from mixing topics, not keeping them separate.

PRACTICE:
Write a short paragraph (5-6 sentences) introducing yourself, your family,
your job, and your plans for next year, deliberately pulling vocabulary
from at least four different earlier lessons.

LESSON 43: Idiomatic Expressions That Make You Sound Italian
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Beyond grammar and vocabulary, a handful of idiomatic expressions do a lot
of work in everyday Italian conversation. Using them naturally is one of
the fastest ways to sound less like a textbook and more like a native
speaker.

GRAMMAR:
- These expressions are best learned as fixed chunks rather than
  analyzed word-by-word — like English idioms ("break a leg," "piece of
  cake"), their meaning doesn't always follow literally from the
  individual words.
- Several function as complete one-word or short-phrase reactions,
  standing in for a full sentence: Mamma mia! (an all-purpose exclamation
  of surprise, frustration, delight — not literally about anyone's
  mother), Magari! (a wistful "if only!" / "I wish!"), Boh! (a shrugging
  "who knows!"), Uffa! (an exhale of annoyance or boredom).
- Others are full idiomatic phrases with a figurative meaning distinct
  from a literal translation: In bocca al lupo! (literally "in the
  wolf's mouth," meaning "good luck!" — the traditional reply is Crepi!
  or Crepi il lupo!, "may the wolf die!," never "grazie," which is
  considered to bring bad luck). Acqua in bocca! (literally "water in
  [your] mouth," meaning "keep it secret," similar to "mum's the word").

VOCABULARY:
Mamma mia! (MAHM-mah MEE-ah) - Oh my!/Wow! (general strong reaction)
Magari! (mah-GAH-ree) - If only!/I wish!
Boh! (boh) - Who knows!/Beats me!
Uffa! (OOF-fah) - Ugh!/How annoying!
In bocca al lupo! (een BOHK-kah ahl LOO-poh) - Good luck!
Crepi! (KREH-pee) - (traditional reply to "good luck") thanks!
Acqua in bocca! (AHK-kwah een BOHK-kah) - Keep it secret!
Che macello! (keh mah-CHEHL-loh) - What a mess!
Non mi va! (nohn mee vah) - I don't feel like it!
Ti sta bene! (tee stah BEH-neh) - Serves you right! / It suits you!

EXAMPLES:
Mamma mia, che caldo oggi! - MAHM-mah MEE-ah, keh KAHL-doh OHJ-jee -
  Wow, it's so hot today!
"In bocca al lupo per l'esame!" "Crepi!" - een BOHK-kah ahl LOO-poh pehr
  leh-ZAH-meh! KREH-pee - "Good luck on the exam!" "Thanks!"
Ho perso le chiavi di nuovo, che macello! - oh PEHR-soh leh KYAH-vee dee
  NWOH-voh, keh mah-CHEHL-loh - I lost my keys again, what a mess!
Vinci alla lotteria? Magari! - VEEN-chee AHL-lah loht-teh-REE-ah?
  mah-GAH-ree - Are you winning the lottery? If only!

NOTES:
- Never respond to "In bocca al lupo!" with grazie — the fixed, correct
  reply is Crepi! (or the fuller Crepi il lupo!); it's a set exchange, not
  a normal thank-you situation.
- Sprinkle a few of these into conversation rather than forcing all of
  them in at once — Mamma mia and Magari are especially natural and
  low-risk starting points, since they work in almost any emotionally
  charged context.

PRACTICE:
Write short one-line reactions using three different expressions from this
lesson to three different imagined situations (good news, bad news, and a
wish).

LESSON 44: False Friends and Classic Mistakes to Avoid
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This final lesson collects the most common traps for English-speaking
learners of Italian: words that look like English but mean something
different, and habits worth breaking early. Think of it as a checklist to
revisit periodically as you continue studying.

GRAMMAR:
- "False friends" (falsi amici) are words that resemble an English word
  but carry a different meaning — the opposite of the reliable cognates
  from Lesson 3. Some of the most common:
    simpatico/a -> does NOT mean "sympathetic"; it means "nice, likeable,
      pleasant" (someone you enjoy being around)
    sensibile -> does NOT mean "sensible"; it means "sensitive." For
      "sensible" (reasonable), use ragionevole instead.
    educato/a -> does NOT mean "educated" (in the sense of schooling); it
      means "well-mannered, polite." For "education" (schooling), use
      istruzione.
    vecchio/a -> means "old," but is typically reserved for objects, not
      people; for describing an older PERSON respectfully, use anziano/a
      instead.
    parenti -> does NOT mean "parents" (that's genitori); parenti means
      "relatives" broadly (aunts, uncles, cousins, etc.).
- A grammar habit worth double-checking: the verb "to play" splits into
  giocare (games/sports) and suonare (instruments) — see Lesson 32 — this
  remains one of the most persistent errors even at intermediate levels.
- A cultural/pragmatic note rather than a strict grammar rule: literal,
  word-for-word translation from English often produces grammatically
  correct but unnatural-sounding Italian. When in doubt, favor the
  shorter, more direct phrasing you've seen modeled throughout this
  course (e.g., Ti piace Roma? rather than a more literal, padded
  translation of "How do you like Rome?").

VOCABULARY:
simpatico/a (seem-PAH-tee-koh) - nice, likeable (NOT "sympathetic")
sensibile (sehn-SEE-bee-leh) - sensitive (NOT "sensible")
ragionevole (rah-joh-NEH-voh-leh) - sensible, reasonable
educato/a (eh-doo-KAH-toh) - polite, well-mannered (NOT "educated")
istruzione (ee-stroo-TSYOH-neh) - education, schooling
anziano/a (ahn-TSYAH-noh) - elderly (used respectfully for people)
i parenti (ee pah-REHN-tee) - relatives (NOT "parents")
i genitori (ee jeh-nee-TOH-ree) - parents
affamato/a (ahf-fah-MAH-toh) - starving, very hungry
in gamba (een GAHM-bah) - sharp, capable (idiomatic, said of a person)

EXAMPLES:
Il mio collega è molto simpatico. - eel MEE-oh kohl-LEH-gah eh MOHL-toh
  seem-PAH-tee-koh - My colleague is very nice/likeable.
Non è vecchio, è anziano — abbi rispetto! - nohn eh VEHK-kyoh, eh
  ahn-TSYAH-noh — AHB-bee rees-PEHT-toh - He's not "old," he's elderly —
  show some respect!
Tutti i miei parenti vengono per Natale. - TOOT-tee ee MYEH-ee
  pah-REHN-tee VEHN-goh-noh pehr nah-TAH-leh - All my relatives are
  coming for Christmas.
È una persona molto in gamba. - eh OO-nah pehr-SOH-nah MOHL-toh een
  GAHM-bah - She's a very sharp, capable person.

NOTES:
- Keep a running personal list of false friends as you encounter new
  ones in reading or conversation — they're highly individual to each
  learner's native language background, and English speakers hit this
  particular set (simpatico, sensibile, educato, parenti) more often than
  most.
- This lesson closes the course, but real fluency comes from continued
  exposure — reading, listening, and above all speaking. Revisit earlier
  lessons' vocabulary in new combinations rather than treating any single
  lesson as "finished" once read.

PRACTICE:
Correct these mismatches: "My parents are visiting" (careful: parenti or
genitori?); "She's very sensible" (careful: sensibile or ragionevole?);
"He's well-educated" (careful: educato or istruito?).
