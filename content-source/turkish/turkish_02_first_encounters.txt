LESSON 4: Is There Any Bread? — Greetings, Var/Yok, and Basic Questions
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers your very first real Turkish grammar: greetings and
farewells, how to make a noun plural, how to turn a statement into a
yes/no question, how to say "there is/there isn't," and how to count to
ten. By the end, you'll be able to walk into a shop, greet the shopkeeper,
ask whether they have something, and describe it with a simple adjective —
which is exactly the situation this lesson's dialogue is built around (a
tourist buying bread, sausage, and olives at a small grocer's).

GRAMMAR:
- Greetings and farewells:
    günaydın (good morning) - only used in the morning
    iyi günler (good day) - a flexible daytime greeting or farewell
    iyi akşamlar (good evening) - from late afternoon onwards
    merhaba (hello) - safe at any time of day
    iyi geceler (good night)
    hoşça kalın (goodbye) - safe at any time, and the easiest to remember
- Plurals: Turkish nouns are made plural by adding -ler or -lar (two-way
  vowel harmony — see Lesson 2). Choose whichever rhymes with the last
  vowel of the word: -ler after e-type (front) vowels, -lar after a-type
  (back) vowels.
    gün (day) -> günler (days)
    akşam (evening) -> akşamlar (evenings)
  Turkish sometimes uses a plural where English uses a singular set
  phrase (iyi günler, "good day," is literally "good days"). Conversely,
  Turkish uses a SINGULAR noun after a number, where English uses a
  plural: iki gün (two days), not "iki günler."
- The question particle mı/mi/mu/mü: to turn a statement into a yes/no
  question, add this particle as a separate word after the item being
  questioned. It follows four-way vowel harmony (Lesson 2), matching the
  last vowel of the preceding word.
    İskoçyalı mı? - Is he Scottish?
    İstanbul mu? - Is it İstanbul?
    Türk mü? - Is he Turkish?
- Var and yok: var means "there is/are," and yok means "there isn't/aren't."
  These two words are among the most useful in the whole language.
    Ekmek var mı? - Is there any bread?
    Çay var. - There is tea.
    Problem yok! - No problem!
- Değil (negation): to make a simple statement negative, place değil after
  the noun or adjective you want to negate.
    Salam değil, sucuk. - It's not salami, it's garlic sausage.
    Hava sıcak değil. - The weather isn't hot.
  Tag onto any statement with Değil mi? to mean "isn't it? / aren't they?"
  — functioning much like French n'est-ce pas?
    Bu sucuk güzel, değil mi? - This garlic sausage is nice, isn't it?
- Adjectives simply sit before the noun they describe, with no agreement
  needed (unlike French or Italian, Turkish adjectives never change form
  for gender or number): güzel elma (nice apple), güzel elmalar (nice
  apples) — güzel stays exactly the same either way.
- Numbers 0-10: sıfır, bir, iki, üç, dört, beş, altı, yedi, sekiz, dokuz, on.

EXAMPLES:
Merhaba. Ekmek var mı? (mehr-hah-BAH. ek-MEK vahr muh) - Hello. Is there
  any bread?

  please.
Bu ne? Salam mı? Salam değil, sucuk. (boo neh? sah-LAHM muh? sah-LAHM
  deh-EEL, soo-JOOK) - What's this? Is it salami? It's not salami, it's
  garlic sausage.

NOTES:
This unit's opening dialogue is monosyllabic on purpose — the book's point
is that you can get remarkably far in Turkish using nothing but var, yok,
ne, and mı/mi/mu/mü! Also worth knowing: it's polite in Turkey to say no
simply through body language — raising your eyebrows with a single soft
"tut" sound — rather than a spoken hayır. And don't be thrown by
sentences like Salam değil, sucuk (literally "not salami, garlic
sausage") — Turkish is comfortable leaving out words like "it" or "is"
that English insists on.

PRACTICE:
1. Turn these statements into questions: (a) Bu Türk. (b) Kahve sıcak.
2. Make these nouns plural: gece (night), televizyon, polis, Türk.
3. Answer with var or yok as if you were the shopkeeper: Süt var mı?
   (assume there is milk); Peynir var mı? (assume there is no cheese).
4. Translate: "The weather isn't cold, is it?"
5. Count aloud from sıfır to on.

LESSON 5: Are You English? — Personal Pronouns and the Verb "To Be"
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers how Turkish handles am/is/are, since Turkish has no
separate verb "to be" the way English does — instead, it attaches personal
endings directly onto the noun or adjective. You'll also learn the
personal pronouns, how to keep vowels apart with buffer consonants,
numbers beyond ten, the three Turkish words for "this/that," and the core
set of question words.

GRAMMAR:
- Personal pronouns: ben (I), sen (you, informal singular), o (he/she/it),
  biz (we), siz (you, formal or plural), onlar (they). Siz is used for
  more than one person, or for a single person you're addressing formally
  — when in doubt, use siz.
- The verb "to be" as endings: instead of a separate word, Turkish attaches
  the endings -im, -sin, (nothing), -iz, -siniz, -ler directly onto the
  noun or adjective. These endings follow four-way vowel harmony (so you'll
  see -im/-ım/-um/-üm, -sin/-sın/-sun/-sün, etc.), except for the "they"
  form -ler, which follows two-way harmony and is usually left out
  entirely.
    ben İngilizim - I am English
    sen İngilizsin - you are English
    o İngiliz - he/she is English (no ending at all)
    biz İngiliziz - we are English
    siz İngilizsiniz - you are English
    onlar İngiliz(ler) - they are English (the -ler is usually dropped)
  You usually drop the personal pronoun itself, since the ending already
  shows who you mean: İngilizim is enough to say "I am English." The
  exception is onlar (they) — you generally keep it, to avoid ambiguity.
- Questions with "to be": split the word into the noun/adjective and its
  ending, then insert mı/mi/mu/mü between them.
    İngilizsiniz -> İngiliz siniz -> İngiliz misiniz? (Are you English?)
  Note the buffer -y- inserted before endings that start with a vowel:
  Güzelim (I'm beautiful) becomes Güzel miyim? (not "Güzel mim?").
- Buffer consonants: Turkish never lets two vowels collide inside a word.
  When an ending starting with a vowel is added to something ending in a
  vowel, insert -y- to keep the pronunciation smooth (you'll meet the
  buffer -n- in a later lesson). Loanwords like maalesef ("unfortunately")
  are rare exceptions where two vowels do sit together.
- Addressing people: use Bey (Mr) and Hanım (Mrs/Miss) AFTER a first name,
  not a surname: John Bey (Mr. Smith / literally "Mr. John"), Lucille
  Hanım. You can also attach Bey to a job title as a polite way to get
  someone's attention: şoför bey! (driver!), memur bey (officer). Use
  beyefendi/hanımefendi (or efendim for either) with strangers whose name
  you don't know.
- Numbers beyond ten: yirmi (20), otuz (30), kırk (40), elli (50), altmış
  (60), yetmiş (70), seksen (80), doksan (90), yüz (100), bin (1,000),
  milyon (1,000,000), milyar (1,000,000,000). Build bigger numbers by
  simply stringing the pieces together: onsekiz (18), ikiyüzkırkbeş
  (245). Unlike English, Turkish doesn't say "a/one hundred" — just yüz.
- Bu, şu, o ("this/that"): where English has two words, Turkish has three.
    bu - this (close to you)
    şu - that (nearby, but not in your hand)
    o - that (further away)
  The line between şu and o is a judgment call, not a hard rule.
- Question words: kim? (who?), ne? (what?), nerede? (where?), ne zaman?
  (when?, literally "what time?"), nasıl? (how?), kaç tane? (how many?),
  ne kadar? (how much?, literally "what extent?").
- The possessive endings -im (my) and -iniz (your): attach directly to a
  noun, following four-way vowel harmony. Ceketim güzel (My jacket is
  nice); Adınız ne? (What's your name?). If the noun ends in a vowel, use
  just -m or -nız.

NOTES:

form to use with a stranger, siz is always the safe choice. Buyurun is a
word you'll hear constantly in Turkey — it's a flexible, all-purpose
prompt meaning roughly "here you are," "go ahead," or "what can I do for
you?" depending on context.

LESSON 6: No More, Please! — Commands, Direct Objects, and Verb Basics
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson takes you into a restaurant, where you'll learn how to give
commands (both casual and formal), how to say "at/in/on," "to," and
"from," how to mark the direct object of a sentence, how Turkish handles
proper nouns and certain consonant changes, and how to find a verb's
dictionary form — the foundation you'll need for every verb tense to come.

GRAMMAR:
- Turkish word order: unlike English, the verb comes at the end of a
  Turkish sentence, and a word that describes another word comes BEFORE
  it. soğuk bir şişe beyaz şarap literally reads "cold a bottle white
  wine" but means "a cold bottle of white wine." When speaking casually,
  don't stress too much about perfect word order — meaning usually comes
  through regardless.
- Commands (imperative): Turkish has three levels of formality.
    Informal: just the bare verb stem. Gel! (Come!) Koy! (Put!)
    Formal: add -in (four-way harmony) to the informal form. Gelin!
    Very formal/official (signs, notices): add -iniz. Geliniz!
  To make a command negative, add -me (two-way harmony) before the
  formality ending: Gelme! (Don't come!) Gelmeyin! (formal — note the
  buffer -y- to stop the vowels clashing) Yapmayınız! (very formal).
- The endings -de (at/in/on), -e (to), and -den (from): these attach to
  nouns and follow two-way vowel harmony.
    Masada otur. - Sit at the table.
    Bakkala git. - Go to the grocer's.
    Bankadan bakkala git. - Go from the bank to the grocer's.
  Note the consonant change: -de becomes -te after an unvoiced consonant
  (ç, f, h, k, p, s, ş, t) — sokakta (in the street), not sokakda.
- Direct objects and the accusative -i: a sentence's direct object is the
  thing an action is done TO. If that object is a SPECIFIC, particular
  thing (the equivalent of English "the"), add the ending -i (four-way
  harmony; -yi if the noun ends in a vowel).
    Kahve iç. - Have a coffee. (any coffee)
    Kahveyi iç. - Drink the coffee. (a specific coffee)
  If the object isn't specific, use no ending, or use bir ("a"):
  Masadan kalem al (Take a pen from the table) vs. Masadan kalemi al
  (Take the pen from the table).
- Personal pronouns take these same endings, and several are irregular:
    ben -> beni, bana, bende, benden
    sen -> seni, sana, sende, senden
    o -> onu, ona, onda, ondan (note the buffer -n- with o)
    biz -> bizi, bize, bizde, bizden
    siz -> sizi, size, sizde, sizden
    onlar -> onları, onlara, onlarda, onlardan
  Note ben+e becomes bana (not "bene"), and sen+e becomes sana — these
  two are simply irregular and worth memorizing on their own.
- Proper nouns: when adding an ending to the name of a specific place or
  person, insert an apostrophe before the ending: Türkiye'de (in Turkey),
  İstanbul'dan İzmir'e (from İstanbul to İzmir).
- Consonant changes: two patterns to know.
    d -> t: the -de/-den endings become -te/-ten after an unvoiced
    consonant (maçta, "at the match"; sokakta, "in the street").
    k -> ğ: a word-final k with a vowel before it changes to ğ when you
    add a vowel-starting ending: kazak -> Kazağı giy! (Put the coat on!)
    This does NOT happen if the letter before k isn't a vowel: park stays
    park (Parka git! - Go to the park!).
- The dictionary form of verbs (the infinitive): Turkish informal commands
  use the bare stem of a verb; the dictionary form is that stem plus -mek
  or -mak (two-way harmony). bak (look!) -> bakmak (to look); ver (give!)
  -> vermek (to give).

  - A table for two, please.

  LEWT-fen) - Bring some ice. Don't put any more, please.

NOTES:

(meze) unprompted and start unloading dishes without being asked — the
custom is to interrupt them, pick what you want, and send the rest back
(daha koymayın, "don't put any more," is exactly the phrase for this).
Also keep in mind that word order matters less for meaning in spoken
Turkish than it does in written Turkish — as long as the endings are
right, listeners will generally understand you even if the words come in
an unusual order.

PRACTICE:

   and why doesn't the same happen to park?
5. Give the infinitive (dictionary form) of: gel, al, otur.

LESSON 7: You're Joking! — The Present Continuous and Possessives
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson introduces the tense you'll use constantly in everyday Turkish
conversation: the -iyor present tense, used for what's happening right
now, for habitual actions, and even for near-future plans. You'll also
learn how to say what belongs to whom (possessives), the endings -li and
-siz ("with" and "without"), and the essential verb istemek ("to want").

GRAMMAR:
- The -iyor present tense: this is Turkish's equivalent of the English
  "-ing" form, though its uses are broader (see below). To build it:
    1. Take the verb stem (e.g. iç-, "drink")
    2. Add -iyor (always -iyor in spelling, though it harmonizes in sound:
       -ıyor/-iyor/-uyor/-üyor depending on the stem's last vowel — four-
       way harmony)
    3. Add the personal ending (same family as "to be": -um, -sun, none,
       -uz, -sunuz, -lar)
    içiyorum - I am drinking (literally "drink-ing I-am")
    geliyorum - I am coming
    satıyorsun - you are selling
    sürüyor - she is driving
    yazıyoruz - we are writing
    koşuyorlar - they are running
- Negative -iyor forms: insert -mi- (which loses its vowel before -iyor,
  becoming just -m-) after the stem: geliyorum -> gelmiyorum (I'm not
  coming). Stress shifts onto the syllable right before -mi- in negative
  forms — this stress shift is just as important as the ending itself for
  being understood.
- Questions with -iyor: split the word into the verb+iyor part and the
  personal ending, then insert the question particle between them.
    geliyorsunuz -> geliyor musunuz? (are you coming?)
  Note that the "they" form is the odd one out, putting mı at the very
  end: koşuyorlar mı? (are they running?)
- Avoiding vowel clashes in -iyor forms: if a verb stem ends in a vowel,
  that vowel is dropped before adding -iyor, to avoid two vowels colliding.
    başlamak (to start) -> başlıyorum (I'm starting), not "başlayıyorum"
    beklemek (to wait) -> bekliyorsunuz (you're waiting)
    yemek (to eat) -> yiyor (he's eating)
  In the negative, though, the stem stays intact, since -mi- starts with a
  consonant sound in context: Başlamıyorum (I'm not starting).
- Uses of the -iyor present tense: not just "right now" — also for stating
  unchanging facts (Sigara kullanmıyorum - I don't smoke), habitual or
  repeated actions (Sık sık plaja gidiyorum - I often go to the beach),
  and near-future plans (Bu akşam geliyorum - I'm coming this evening).
- Possessives — the possessor ending -in: to say something belongs to
  someone, add -in (or -nin after a vowel) to the possessor, following
  four-way harmony.
    kızın - the girl's        annenin - mother's
- Possessives — the possessed ending -i/-si: Turkish ALSO marks the thing
  being possessed, unlike English. Add -i (or -si after a vowel).
    doktorun evi - the doctor's house
    kızların annesi - the girls' mother
  Exception: su (water) takes -yu instead of -su: Ali'nin suyu (Ali's
  water).
- Possessive personal pronouns: benim (my), senin (your), onun (his/her),
  bizim (our), sizin (your), onların (their) — note ben and biz are
  slightly irregular, taking -im rather than -in. The possessed noun then
  takes the matching ending: benim evim (my home), onun evi (his/her
  home). Because the possessed ending already shows the owner, you can
  usually drop the pronoun itself — except onların, which is best kept to
  avoid confusing "his" with "their."
- Saying what you've got: Turkish uses var/yok with a possessive rather
  than a separate verb "to have." Kızım var. (I've got a daughter —
  literally "my daughter exists.") Arabası yok mu? (Doesn't he have a
  car?)
- The endings -li and -siz ("with" and "without"): these make adjectives,
  following four-way (-li) and two-way (-siz) harmony respectively.
    balkonlu - with a balcony        şekersiz - without sugar
  -li also attaches to country and town names to give a nationality or
  demonym: Kanada -> Kanadalı (Canadian); İstanbul -> İstanbullu (a
  person from İstanbul). Some nationalities are irregular exceptions:
  Japonya -> Japon (not "Japonyalı"); Rusya -> Rus.
- İstemek (to want): use it with a noun (Bir kilo domates istiyorum - I
  want a kilo of tomatoes) or with an infinitive verb (Gitmek istiyorum -
  I want to go).

EXAMPLES:
İçiyor musunuz? Teşekkür ederim, sigara kullanmıyorum. (ee-CHEE-yohr
  moo-soo-NOOZ? teh-shek-KEWR eh-deh-REEM, see-gah-RAH kool-lah-NUH-yoh-room)
  - Do you smoke? Thank you, I don't smoke.
Kilimlere bakmak istiyorum. (kee-leem-leh-REH bahk-MAHK ees-tee-YOH-room)
  - I want to look at the rugs.
Kazağı giy! Sucuğu ye! (kah-zah-UH giy! soo-joo-OO yeh!) - Put the coat
  on! Eat the garlic sausage!
Kızım var. Odanın banyosu var mı? (kuh-ZUHM vahr. oh-dah-nuhn bahn-yoh-SOO
  vahr muh) - I've got a daughter. Does the room have a bath?
Uzun ömürlü süt istiyorum. (oo-ZOON ur-mewr-LEW sewt ees-tee-YOH-room) -
  I want long-life milk.

NOTES:
When someone offers you something in Turkey, saying teşekkür ederim
("thank you") on its own is generally taken to mean "no thank you" — to
accept, use lütfen instead. This can genuinely cause confusion (and very
hungry foreigners) at a Turkish dinner table! Also note ben de and şu de
— de/da means "also/too," which is different from the endings -de/-da
that mean "in/at/on"; context makes clear which is meant.

PRACTICE:
1. Put these verbs into the -iyor present, "I" form: gelmek (to come),
   yazmak (to write), yemek (to eat, watch the vowel clash).
2. Make these negative: geliyorum, satıyorsun, yiyor.
3. Translate using a possessive: "the firm's director" and "my mother."
4. Say "I don't have a car" and "Do you have milk?" using var/yok.
5. Turn Kanada and İstanbul into nationality/demonym adjectives using -li.
