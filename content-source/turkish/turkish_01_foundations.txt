LESSON 1: The Turkish Alphabet and Sounds
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Turkish spelling is almost perfectly phonetic: every letter stands for
exactly one sound, that sound never changes, and you always pronounce every
letter in a word. This is very good news — once you know the alphabet, you
can correctly pronounce any Turkish word you see, even one you've never
encountered before. The Turkish alphabet has 29 letters: it's the same as
the English alphabet but drops Q, W and X, and adds six extra letters
(ç, ğ, ı, ö, ş, ü) not found in English.

GRAMMAR:
- The Turkish alphabet: A a, B b, C c, Ç ç, D d, E e, F f, G g, Ğ ğ, H h,
  I ı, İ i, J j, K k, L l, M m, N n, O o, Ö ö, P p, R r, S s, Ş ş, T t, U u,
  Ü ü, V v, Y y, Z z.
- Turkish vowels are always "short" — a Turkish u sounds like the u in the
  northern English "put," never like the oo-glide in "super." English
  speakers tend to draw Turkish vowels out too long, so aim for something
  crisp and short.
- The vowels English also has:
    a - like the a in "cat" (northern English) or "cup" (southern English)
    e - like the e in "bet"
    i (with a dot, even capitalized: İ) - like the i in "sit"
    o - like the o in "hot"
    u - like the u in "put"
- The vowels English does NOT have:
    ı (no dot, even capitalized as I) - like the "er" in "butter," or the u
      in "cousin" — a neutral, unstressed-sounding vowel
    ö - like the German ö, or the "eu" in French "deux" — round your lips
      as if to say "o" but say "e"
    ü - like the German ü, or the French u in "tu" — round your lips as if
      to say "u" but say "i" — a sound halfway between English "ee" and "oo"
- Crucially: dotted İ/i and dotless I/ı are two completely different
  letters in Turkish, not capitalization variants of each other. This trips
  up almost every beginner, so it's worth fixing in your mind right away:
  İstanbul is spelled with a dotted İ, and its capital I keeps the dot.
- Consonants that sound different from English:
    c - like the j in "John" (NOT like an English c)
    ç - like the ch in "child"
    j - like the s in "pleasure," or the j in French "Jacques"
    ş - like the sh in "show"
    v - a sound halfway between English v and w
    ğ (yumuşak g, "soft g") - has NO sound of its own. It always follows a
      vowel and lengthens that vowel instead — think of dağ (mountain) as
      "daa," or öğle (noon) as a lengthened "öö-leh." No Turkish word ever
      begins with ğ.
- Unlike English, Turkish always pronounces every letter — there's no
  silent h (as in English "hour") and no dropped final r (as in
  non-rhotic English accents). Every letter earns its place.
- Word stress: as a general rule, stress falls on the LAST syllable of a
  word (kapı - kah-PUH, "door"). There are exceptions — mostly place names
  and a handful of loanwords — which you'll meet as they come up.

VOCABULARY:
kapı (kah-PUH) - door
ekmek (ek-MEK) - bread
güzel (gew-ZEL) - nice, beautiful
öğretmen (ur-ret-MEN) - teacher
çocuk (choh-JOOK) - child
şehir (sheh-HEER) - city
üzüm (ew-ZEWM) - grape
ığdır (uhg-DUHR) - (a place name; example of ı)
yağmur (yah-MOOR) - rain
gün (gewn) - day

EXAMPLES:
Kapı büyük. (kah-PUH bew-YEWK) - The door is big.
Çocuk güzel. (choh-JOOK gew-ZEL) - The child is beautiful.
Bugün yağmur var. (boo-GEWN yah-MOOR var) - There is rain today.
Öğretmen nerede? (ur-ret-MEN neh-reh-DEH) - Where is the teacher?

NOTES:
Because Turkish spelling is so consistent, it's worth spending real time on
this lesson before moving on — every other lesson in this course assumes
you can sound out new words correctly on sight. If you're ever unsure how
to pronounce a word later in the course, come back to this lesson's sound
list. Keep an eye out especially for ı vs i and for ğ, since these are the
two features English speakers most often get wrong at first.

PRACTICE:
1. Read these words aloud, then check yourself against the phonetics:
   sokak (street), köpek (dog), balık (fish), üniversite (university),
   yaş (age).
2. Circle the "extra" Turkish letters (not found in English) in this
   sentence: Öğrencinin çantası şurada.
3. True or false: dotless ı and dotted i are just capital/lowercase forms
   of the same letter.
4. Explain in your own words what the letter ğ does to the vowel before it.
5. Which syllable is normally stressed in a Turkish word: first, middle,
   or last?

================================================================
LESSON 2: Vowel Harmony and Word Stress
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Vowel harmony is the single most important pattern in Turkish grammar, and
it will come up in almost every lesson from here on. Turkish endings
(suffixes) usually come in either two or four different versions, and which
version you use depends on the last vowel of the word you're attaching the
ending to. This lesson gives you the full system up front so that later
lessons can simply say "this ending follows two-way harmony" or "this
ending follows four-way harmony" without re-explaining it each time.

GRAMMAR:
- Turkish vowels divide into two families based on where in the mouth
  they're produced:
    FRONT vowels: e, i, ö, ü
    BACK vowels:  a, ı, o, u
- Two-way vowel harmony ("e-type" harmony): an ending has two versions,
  one with e and one with a. You choose based on the LAST vowel of the
  word:
    - if the last vowel is a front vowel (e, i, ö, ü) -> use the e version
    - if the last vowel is a back vowel (a, ı, o, u) -> use the a version
  Example: the plural ending -ler/-lar works this way.
    ev (house, back... wait, e is front) -> evler (houses)
    araba (car, back vowel a) -> arabalar (cars)
- Four-way vowel harmony ("i-type" harmony): an ending has four versions,
  matching not just front/back but also rounded/unrounded:
    - last vowel i (front, unrounded) -> use the i version
    - last vowel ü (front, rounded) -> use the ü version
    - last vowel ı (back, unrounded) -> use the ı version
    - last vowel u (back, rounded) -> use the u version
  Example: the question particle mı/mi/mu/mü works this way.
    İstanbul mu? (Is it İstanbul?) - last vowel u -> mu
    Türk mü? (Is he Turkish?) - last vowel ü -> mü
    Paris mi? (Is it Paris?) - last vowel i -> mi
    Roma mı? (Is it Rome?) - last vowel a (back, unrounded, closest match
      to ı) -> mı
- A simple way to remember the four-way pattern: the vowel of the ending
  "rhymes" with the last vowel of the word. If the exact vowel isn't
  available (e.g. the word ends in e, a, ö or o), pick whichever of the
  four options sounds closest.
- You do not need to memorize these rules by rote. As you listen to and
  read more Turkish, harmonizing correctly becomes instinctive. And even if
  you get it wrong as a beginner, Turks will still understand you fine —
  vowel harmony affects how natural you sound, not whether you're understood.
- Consonant harmony: Turkish also "harmonizes" certain consonants for ease
  of pronunciation. You'll meet the two main patterns in later lessons:
    - voiced/voiceless matching: the d in endings like -de or -den becomes
      t after a word ending in an unvoiced consonant (ç, f, h, k, p, s, ş,
      t) — e.g. sokakta (in the street), not sokakda.
    - k -> ğ: a word-final k preceded by a vowel changes to ğ when you add
      an ending that starts with (or is) a vowel — e.g. kazak (coat) ->
      kazağı (its coat / the coat, object form).
- Buffer consonants: Turkish never allows two vowels to sit next to each
  other inside a word. When an ending that starts with a vowel is added to
  a word that ends in a vowel, a "buffer" consonant is inserted between
  them to keep the pronunciation clean. The most common buffers are -y-,
  -n- and -s-; you'll learn exactly which one to use for which ending as
  each ending is introduced.
- Word stress recap: the default is to stress the last syllable
  (kapı - kah-PUH). A few categories of words break this rule — including
  most place names (this book's convention: stress marked in phonetics as
  it comes up) and words borrowed from Arabic or Persian with a naturally
  "long" vowel (like lazım, necessary, pronounced with a lengthened first
  a: LAA-zuhm).

VOCABULARY:
ev (ev) - house
araba (ah-rah-BAH) - car
oda (oh-DAH) - room
göz (gurz) - eye
kulak (koo-LAHK) - ear
sokak (soh-KAHK) - street
kitap (kee-TAHP) - book
kazak (kah-ZAHK) - coat, sweater
lazım (LAA-zuhm) - necessary
hâlâ (hah-LAH) - still (an example of the circumflex marking a lengthened
  Arabic-origin vowel)

EXAMPLES:
Evler büyük. (ev-LER bew-YEWK) - The houses are big.
Arabalar güzel. (ah-rah-bah-LAR gew-ZEL) - The cars are nice.
Kitap mı? (kee-TAHP muh) - Is it a book?
Göz mü? (gurz mew) - Is it an eye?
Kazağı giy! (kah-zah-UH giy) - Put the coat on! (k changes to ğ)

NOTES:
Don't try to consciously calculate vowel harmony while speaking at first —
it will slow you down and isn't necessary to be understood. Instead, notice
the pattern passively as you go through this course: nearly every plural,
question, and personal ending you meet will explicitly tell you whether it
follows two-way or four-way harmony, and over time the correct forms will
simply start to feel right.

PRACTICE:
1. Sort these words by whether their last vowel is front or back:
   masa (table), sandalye (chair), kapı (door), gözlük (glasses).
2. Add the plural ending (-ler or -lar, following two-way harmony) to:
   kitap, göz, ev, sokak.
3. Which version of mı/mi/mu/mü would follow the word süt (milk)?
4. Explain why sokakta (in the street) uses -ta rather than -da.
5. What is a "buffer consonant" and why does Turkish need one?

================================================================
LESSON 3: Survival Turkish — Essential Words and Phrases
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Before diving into full sentences and grammar, it helps enormously to have
a stock of ready-made words and phrases you can lean on right away — the
kind of language that gets you through a shop, a taxi, or a hotel check-in
even before you can build your own sentences. This lesson is a practical
phrasebook covering the situations you'll run into most often in Turkey.

GRAMMAR:
- Most of this lesson is vocabulary rather than grammar, but a few patterns
  are worth flagging early:
    - lütfen (please) goes at the end (or occasionally the start) of a
      request: Bir çay, lütfen. (One tea, please.)
    - Turkish often omits words that English needs, like "a/an" or "is" —
      var mı? literally means "is there?" with nothing standing in for "a."
    - Yes/no answers frequently repeat the key word rather than using a
      simple "yes": Ekmek var mı? - Var. (Is there bread? - There is.)
- A note on politeness: raising your eyebrows and making a single soft
  "tut" sound is a normal, polite way to say no in Turkey — it isn't rude
  or dismissive the way it might seem to an English speaker.

VOCABULARY:
Basics:
evet (eh-VET) - yes
hayır (hah-YUHR) - no
lütfen (LEWT-fen) - please
sağ olun (saa oh-LOON) - thank you
merhaba (mehr-hah-BAH) - hello
hoşça kalın (hosh-CHAH kah-LUHN) - goodbye
nasılsınız? (nah-suhl-suh-NUHZ) - how are you?
iyiyim (ee-YEE-yeem) - I'm fine
pardon (pahr-DOHN) - excuse me

Talking:
anladım (ahn-lah-DUHM) - I understand
anlamadım (ahn-lah-mah-DUHM) - I don't understand
tekrar (tek-RAHR) - again
yavaş (yah-VAHSH) - slowly
ne demek? (neh deh-MEK) - what does it mean?
İngilizce (in-gee-leez-JEH) - English (language)
Türkçesi ne? (tewrk-cheh-SEE neh) - what is it in Turkish?

Shopping:
kaç para? (kach pah-RAH) - how much (money)?
ucuz (oo-JOOZ) - cheap
pahalı (pah-hah-LUH) - expensive
var (vahr) - there is (some)
yok (yohk) - there isn't (any)

Eating:
bakar mısınız! (bah-KAHR muh-suh-NUHZ) - waiter! excuse me! (literally
  "would you look!")
hesap (heh-SAHP) - the bill
öğle yemeği (ur-LEH yeh-meh-EE) - lunch

Directions:
nerede? (neh-reh-DEH) - where?
sol (sohl) - left
sağ (saa) - right
düz (dewz) - straight on

Travelling:
ne zaman? (neh zah-MAHN) - when?
bilet (bee-LET) - ticket
burada (boo-rah-DAH) - here

Times:
dakika (dah-kee-KAH) - minute
saat (sah-AHT) - hour
şimdi (SHEEM-dee) - now
dün (dewn) - yesterday
bugün (boo-GEWN) - today
yarın (yah-RUHN) - tomorrow

Numbers:
az (ahz) - a little
çok (chohk) - a lot
bir, iki, üç, dört, beş (beer, ee-KEE, ewch, durt, besh) - one, two, three,
  four, five

Places:
tuvalet (too-vah-LET) - toilet
postane (pohs-tah-NEH) - post office
eczane (edj-zah-NEH) - chemist's, pharmacy
bakkal (bahk-KAHL) - grocer's shop

Trouble:
imdat! (eem-DAHT) - help!
kaza (kah-ZAH) - accident
doktor (dohk-TOHR) - doctor

EXAMPLES:
Bir çay, lütfen. (beer chai LEWT-fen) - One tea, please.
Ekmek var mı? - Var. (ek-MEK vahr muh - vahr) - Is there bread? - There is.
Anlamadım. Tekrar, lütfen. (ahn-lah-mah-DUHM, tek-RAHR LEWT-fen) - I don't
  understand. Again, please.
Tuvalet nerede? (too-vah-LET neh-reh-DEH) - Where is the toilet?
Kaç para? (kach pah-RAH) - How much is it?

NOTES:
Don't worry about understanding the grammar behind these phrases yet — that
comes over the next several lessons. For now, the goal is simply
recognition and recall: if you can greet someone, ask if something's
available, ask the price, and say you don't understand, you already have
enough Turkish to get through a huge range of everyday situations. As you
listen to Turkish (audio, TV, conversation), pay attention to these words
in particular — they come up constantly.

PRACTICE:
1. How would you ask "is there any bread?" and then say "there isn't any"?
2. Match each phrase to its meaning: (a) hoşça kalın (b) anlamadım
   (c) kaç para? — (i) I don't understand (ii) how much? (iii) goodbye.
3. You want to ask where the post office is. What do you say?
4. Someone offers you tea you don't want. What is the polite non-verbal way
   to decline, according to this lesson?
5. Translate: "Excuse me, is there a toilet here?"
