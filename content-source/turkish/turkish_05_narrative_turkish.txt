LESSON 16: Could You Tell Me What Happened? — Past Continuous, Pluperfect, and Reported Speech
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson equips you to tell a detailed story about the past: what was
happening at a given moment, what had already happened before that, and
how to report what someone said, using nominalized -dik/-ecek clauses as
the objects of verbs like "say," "hear," or "understand." You'll also
learn how to build nouns from verbs with -iş and a handful of special
expressions using var/yok.

GRAMMAR:
- The past continuous tense ("what was happening"): build it from the
  present continuous (-iyor) by removing the personal ending and adding
  the past form of "to be" instead.
    yapıyorsunuz -> yapıyor -> yapıyordunuz - you were doing
    Saat sekizde ne yapıyordun? - What were you doing at eight o'clock?
    Ders çalışıyordum. - I was studying.
- The pluperfect ("what had happened," -mişti): NOT related in meaning to
  the reported -miş from Lesson 14 — this is simply the equivalent of
  English "had done."
    Eve geldiğim zaman sen gitmiştin. - When I came home, you had gone.
    Daha önce hiç yememiştim. - I had never eaten it before.
- Reporting events with -dik/-ecek nouns: to say "I said that I was
  fine," Turkish uses the -dik (or, for the future, -ecek) participle as
  a noun functioning as the object of the reporting verb — literally
  "fine my-being I-said."
    Hasta olduğumu kim söyledi? - Who said that I was ill?
    Senin bunu söyleyeceğini tahmin ettim. - I guessed you would say
    that.
    Onların boşanacağını sanmam. - I don't reckon they'll get divorced.
- Combining -dik/-ecek nouns with connecting words: certain words attach
  after these participles to build more complex connectors (shown below
  with -diği as the placeholder form):
    -diği gibi - just as             -diği halde - although
    -diği için - due to, because     -diği kadar - as far as
    -diği zaman / -diğinde - when    -diğinden başka - apart from
    Bildiğim kadar iyiler. - As far as I know, they're fine.
    Fakir olduğu halde hayatından memnun. - Although he's poor, he's
    content with his life.
- The difference between -dik/-ecek nouns and the short infinitive: use
  -dik/-ecek nouns for a SPECIFIC event that actually happened or will
  happen; use the short infinitive for the general CONCEPT or manner of
  doing something.
    Şarkı söylediğiniz doğru mu? - Is it true that you sing? (a specific
    fact about you)
    Şarkı söylemeniz doğru mu? - Is your singing the right thing to do?
    (the concept/appropriateness of singing)
- Building nouns with -iş: attach to a verb stem to create a related
  noun, often about an action's occurrence.
    kalkmak (to leave) -> kalkış (departure)
    girmek (to enter) -> giriş (entry)
    uçmak (to fly) -> uçuş (flight)
    almak/vermek (to take/give) -> alışveriş (shopping)
- Special expressions with var and yok:
    Bir varmış bir yokmuş... - Once upon a time... (uses -miş, tying back
    to Lesson 14's storytelling use)
    Ne var? - What's up? What's the matter?
    Ne var ne yok? - What's the news?
    var etmek - to create, to cause to exist
    yok etmek - to get rid of
    var olmak - to exist
    yok olmak - to disappear

EXAMPLES:

  swimming in the sea.

  Who said that I was ill?

  Although he's poor, he's happy.

NOTES:
This unit's dialogue (a tourist reporting a theft to a policeman) is a
good model for real narrative Turkish: notice how it layers the past
continuous ("I was swimming"), the pluperfect ("he had disappeared"), and
reported description together in a single connected account. Practicing
retelling a simple personal story using these three tools together is one
of the most useful exercises at this stage.

PRACTICE:
1. Turn yapıyorsunuz into the past continuous.
2. Say "I had never eaten it before" using -mişti.
3. Report this statement as something someone said: "Ali went home"
   (Ali eve gitti) -> "They said that Ali went home."
4. Build a noun meaning "flight" from uçmak using -iş.
5. Translate: "Although he's poor, he's content with his life," using
   -diği halde.

LESSON 17: Don't Make Me Laugh! — The Habitual Past, Causatives, and Reciprocals
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers how to talk about things you USED to do, how to make
someone else do something (causative verbs), the important difference
between a verb happening to itself versus being done to something else,
how to say "each other," and how Turkish builds adverbs.

GRAMMAR:
- The habitual past ("used to," -erdi): built the same way as the past
  conditional pattern you'll see again in Lesson 19 — take the -r present
  form, strip the personal ending, and add the past "to be" ending.
    içersiniz -> içer -> içerdiniz - you used to drink
    Araba sürerdim. - I used to drive.
    Çok çalışırdılar (or Çok çalışırlardı). - They used to work a lot.
  Negate normally, using the -mez/-maz negative -r present as the base:
  Araba sürmezdim. - I didn't use to drive.
- Causative verbs ("to make/have someone do something"): Turkish adds an
  ending to the verb stem to show that the subject is CAUSING the action
  rather than doing it directly. The most common causative ending is
  -dir, but several variants exist (-t, -it, -ir, -ert, depending on the
  verb):
    ölmek (to die) -> öldürmek (to kill)
    gülmek (to smile) -> güldürmek (to make someone smile)
    hatırlamak (to remember) -> hatırlatmak (to remind)
    korkmak (to be afraid) -> korkutmak (to scare)
    düşmek (to fall) -> düşürmek (to drop)
    çıkmak (to go out) -> çıkarmak (to take out, take off clothing)
  There's no single universal rule for which variant a given verb takes —
  though verbs with more than one syllable that end in r, l, or a vowel
  tend to take -t. Learn each causative pairing as you meet it.
- Transitive vs. intransitive — "stopping something" vs. "just stopping":
  English sometimes uses the same verb both ways ("I stopped" / "I
  stopped the car"), but Turkish generally needs two different verbs: the
  plain form for something happening to itself (intransitive), and the
  causative form for making it happen to something else (transitive).
    Durdum. - I stopped. (intransitive)
    Arabayı durdurdum. - I stopped the car. (transitive/causative)
    Uyan! - Wake up! (intransitive)      Uyandır onu! - Wake him up!
    (causative)
  When deciding how to translate an English verb like "stop," "wake," or
  "grow" into Turkish, always ask: is the subject doing this to itself,
  or causing it to happen to someone/something else?
- Each other — birbiri: this word means "each other" and changes form to
  match the subject: birbirimiz (we... each other), birbiriniz (you...
  each other), birbirleri (they... each other).
    Birbirimizi seviyoruz. - We love each other.
    Birbirinize yardım edin! - Help each other!
  Birbiri is always the object (or part of the object) of its sentence,
  never the subject.
- The -iş ending as "each other": besides the noun-building use from
  Lesson 16, -iş (and its variants) attached directly to a verb stem can
  ALSO mean "each other" — this is called the reciprocal form.
    Yarın görüşürüz. - We'll see each other tomorrow.
- Adverbs — when, where, and how: many Turkish adjectives double as
  adverbs with no change at all: İyi gidiyor. (It's going well.) Türkçeyi
  güzel konuşuyorsunuz. (You speak Turkish well.) You can also build
  adverbs with -ce (Yavaşça sür. - Drive slowly), or by simply repeating
  a word (yavaş yavaş - slowly; zaman zaman - now and then). Adverbs
  normally go right before the verb they describe, and when a sentence
  has more than one adverb, "when" words usually come before "where" or
  "how" words. A useful set of "when" adverbs: artık (now, from now on),
  henüz (not yet), hiçbir zaman (never), bazen (sometimes), sık sık
  (often), her zaman (always).

EXAMPLES:
Okulda çok iyi arkadaştık, değil mi? (oh-kool-DAH chohk ee-YEE
  ahr-kah-dahsh-TUHK, deh-EEL mee) - We were very good friends at school,
  weren't we?
Voleybol oynardım ama artık oynamıyorum. (voh-ley-BOHL oy-nahr-DUHM
  ah-MAH ahr-TUHK oy-nah-muh-yoh-room) - I used to play volleyball but I
  don't play anymore.
Beni güldürme! (beh-NEE gewl-dewr-MEH) - Don't make me laugh!

  tah-nuh-yoh-ROOZ) - We've known each other for years.

  LEWT-fen) - Please speak slowly.

NOTES:
The transitive/intransitive distinction (durmak vs. durdurmak) is one of
the more subtle points in this course, but it becomes intuitive quickly
once you start noticing it: ask "is the subject affecting only itself, or
affecting something else?" before choosing which form of a verb to use.
It's a genuinely different way of thinking about verbs than English uses,
where the same word (like "stop" or "break") often covers both roles.

PRACTICE:
1. Say "I used to smoke" (sigara kullanırdım pattern) using içmek or
   kullanmak.
2. Build the causative of gülmek (to smile) and use it in "Don't make me
   laugh."
3. Choose the right verb: "I woke up" vs. "I woke him up" — uyanmak or
   uyandırmak?
4. Translate "We love each other" using birbiri.
5. Build an adverb from yavaş meaning "slowly, slowly" by repetition.

LESSON 18: When Was This Place Built? — The Passive Voice and Reflexives
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers the passive voice (used far more often in Turkish than
in English, especially on signs), how to say "by" someone, the "was going
to happen" past future tense, and reflexive verbs and pronouns
("myself," "yourself"). You'll finish with a set of noun/adjective-
building endings useful for expanding your vocabulary independently.

GRAMMAR:
- The passive voice: used when you want to focus on what was done or what
  something had done to it, rather than on who did it. Build it by adding
  -il or -in (there are also -n and other variants for certain stem
  shapes) after the verb stem.
    açmak (to open) -> açılmak (to be opened)
    çekmek (to pull) -> çekilmek (to be pulled)
    bulmak (to find) -> bulunmak (to be found)
  Rules for which variant to use:
    most verbs: -il             stems ending in a vowel: -n
    stems ending in l: -in
  Compare an active and passive pair: Cüzdanı çaldı. (He stole the
  purse.) -> Cüzdan çalındı. (The purse was stolen.) Note how the object
  (cüzdan) drops its -i ending and becomes the SUBJECT of the passive
  sentence.
- Saying "by" in a passive sentence — tarafından: place this word after
  the person who performed the action.
    "Hamlet" Sekspir tarafından yazıldı. - "Hamlet" was written by
    Shakespeare.
  With personal pronouns, put a possessor ending on the pronoun and a
  possessed ending in the middle of tarafından: benim tarafımdan (by
  me). This is only used for nouns/pronouns, not attached directly to
  ordinary nouns without tarafından. Also note: Turkish generally reserves
  tarafından passives for inventions, discoveries, or describing a
  process — for everyday statements, an active sentence (Bu resmi ben
  çizdim - I drew this picture) is more natural than the passive.
- The passive on signs: Turkish uses the passive far more than English
  does, especially in public signage.
    Tamir edilir. - Repairs made.        Döviz alınır. - Foreign currency
    accepted.
    Girilmez. - No entry.                Sigara içilmez. - Smoking
    forbidden.
- The past future tense ("was going to," -ecekti): build from the future
  tense by removing the personal ending and adding the past "to be"
  ending instead.
    içeceğim -> içecek -> içecektim - I was going to drink
    Sana telefon edecektim, ama numaranı kaybettim. - I was going to
    telephone you, but I lost your number.
- Reflexives — kendi ("self"): combine with the possessed endings -im,
  -in, -i, -imiz, -iniz, -leri.
    Kendim seçtim. - I chose it myself.
    Kendine iyi bak. - Look after yourself.
  For "by myself," double it: Kendi kendime seçtim. (I chose it all by
  myself.) Kendi can also mean "my own/your own" etc. when combined with
  a possessed noun: Kendi arabamızı kullandık. (We used our own car.)
- Reflexive verbs: add -in to a verb stem to build a reflexive form.
    yıkamak (to wash something) -> yıkanmak (to wash yourself)
    giymek (to put clothes on) -> giyinmek (to get dressed)
  Some reflexive forms take on their own special meaning: dinlemek (to
  listen) -> dinlenmek (to relax, literally "to listen to yourself").
  Watch out — for some verbs the reflexive form looks IDENTICAL to the
  passive form (e.g. taşınmak can mean either "to move house" or "to be
  moved"); context makes the intended meaning clear.
- Vocabulary-building endings: Turkish builds many nouns and adjectives
  from verb stems using a family of endings. A sample set:
    -im: doğmak (to be born) -> doğum (birth)
    -in: sormak (to ask) -> sorun (problem)
    -gin/-kin: üzmek (to hurt feelings) -> üzgün (sad)
    -gen/-ken: çalışmak (to work) -> çalışkan (hard-working)
    -gi: bilmek (to know) -> bilgi (knowledge)
    -i: sormak (to ask) -> soru (question)

conversation.

LESSON 19: If I Had Known... — Unreal Conditionals, Keşke, and Yapar Yapmaz
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This final lesson completes the Turkish conditional system with the
"unreal" and "past" conditional (used for hypothetical or regretful
situations), the word keşke for expressing wishes and regrets, the handy
pattern for saying "as soon as," and a last set of vocabulary-building
endings to round out your ability to recognize new words on your own.

GRAMMAR:
- The second and third conditional (-seydi): in Lesson 13 you learned the
  present conditional (-se combined with the present "to be"). Now
  combine -se- with the PAST tense of "to be" instead to get a second set
  of "if" endings, used for hypothetical or counterfactual situations.
    gelseydim - if I had come / if I came (hypothetically)
    yapsaydın - if you had done / if you did
    görseydiniz - if you had seen / if you saw
  This single form covers two related English meanings, distinguished by
  context:
    "Unreal" conditional (about the future, unlikely to happen):
      Yerinde olsaydım giderdim. - If I were you, I would go.
    Past conditional (about something that might have happened, but
    didn't):
      Yerinde olsaydım giderdim. - If I had been you, I would have gone.
  Note that in BOTH readings, the result clause uses the habitual past
  (-erdi, from Lesson 17) — but here it should be translated as "would
  do" or "would have done," NOT as "used to do." Compare gelirsen memnun
  olurum (if you come, I'll be happy — a simple, real possibility) with
  gelseydin memnun olurdum (if you came/had come, I would be/have been
  happy — expresses doubt about whether it will or did happen).
- Keşke ("if only... / I wish..."): pairs with the past conditional form
  to express regret or a wish.
    Keşke gitmeseydin. - I wish you hadn't gone.
    Keşke daha güzel olsaydım! - If only I were prettier!
    Keşke hiç tanışmasaydık! - I wish we had never met!
- Yapar yapmaz ("as soon as"): place the positive and negative -r present
  forms of the same verb back-to-back.
    Eve gelir gelmez banyo yapacağım. - I'll have a bath the minute I get
    home.
    Telefon çalar çalmaz cevap ver! - Answer straight away when the
    phone rings!
- More vocabulary-building endings: a final set, rounding out the pattern
  from Lesson 18, to help you recognize and eventually build new words
  from verb stems on your own.
    -mez: değişmek (to change) -> değişmez (unchanging, stable)
    -ç: kazanmak (to win) -> kazanç (profit, gain)
    -gıç: başlamak (to start) -> başlangıç (the start)
    -ik: bozmak (to break) -> bozuk (broken)
    -ek: uçmak (to fly) -> uçak (airplane)
    -it: geçmek (to pass) -> geçit (mountain pass, passage)
    -ıntı: akmak (to flow) -> akıntı (current)

these 19 lessons (from var/yok all the way to unreal conditionals) takes

the best next steps are heavy exposure: reading, listening to Turkish
media, and above all speaking — the -miş, -dik, and conditional patterns
in particular only become truly natural through repeated real use.

5. Build a noun from uçmak (to fly) meaning "airplane" using -ek.
