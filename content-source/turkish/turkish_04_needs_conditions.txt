LESSON 12: What Do You Like Doing? — Infinitives as Nouns and Obligation
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson shows you how to use a verb's infinitive as if it were a noun
— essential for talking about likes, dislikes, and abstract activities —
and covers the several ways Turkish expresses obligation ("must,"
"should," "need to"). You'll also meet olmak's special idiomatic phrases
and the noun-building ending -lik.

GRAMMAR:
- Using infinitives as nouns: Turkish infinitives (the -mek/-mak dictionary
  form) can function as nouns, similar to English gerunds ("swimming is
  fun"). There are two versions: the "full" infinitive and a "short"
  version with the final -k dropped.
    Full infinitive — used with adjectives, with istiyorum, and with için:
      Türkçe öğrenmek kolay. - Learning Turkish is easy.
      Konuşmak istiyorum. - I want to talk.
      Oynamak için geldim. - I came in order to play.
    Short infinitive — used as an ordinary noun, including as the first
    half of a compound noun:
      konuşmak -> konuşma - conversation
      dondurmak -> dondurma - ice cream
      yüzme havuzu - swimming pool
    A subtle trap: the short infinitive can look identical to a negative
    command, distinguished only by stress. İÇ-me suyu! (Don't drink the
    water!) vs. iç-ME suyu (drinking water) — remember, negative verb
    forms stress the syllable right before -me.
- Adding endings to infinitives used as nouns:
    -den attaches to the FULL infinitive: Uçmaktan korkuyorum. (I'm
    afraid of flying.)
    -i or -e attach to the SHORT infinitive: Pişirmeyi bıraktı. (He
    stopped cooking.) Koşmaya başladı. (He began to run.)
    Possessed endings also attach to the SHORT infinitive: Benim Türk
    olmam iyi. (It's good that I'm Turkish — literally "my being Turkish
    is good.") You can usually drop the personal pronoun (benim) here,
    though including it makes the meaning clearer.
- Expressing obligation with lazım, gerek, şart, and mecbur: combine any
  of these words with the short infinitive (plus a possessed ending) to
  say what you need, should, or are forced to do.
    Saat sekizde işte olmam lazım. - I need to be at work at eight.
    Saat sekizde işte olması gereklidir. - She needs to be at work at
    eight.
  Şart and mecbur are the strongest of these — closer to "absolutely
  must." There's also a verb, gerekmek, useful for talking about
  obligation in the future or past: Onların işte olması gerekecek. (They
  will have to be at work.) Dün onların işte olması gerekti. (They had
  to be at work yesterday.)
- Expressing obligation with -meli: spot must/should/ought to by -meli
  (two-way harmony) added to the verb stem, then the personal ending.
    Okula gitmelisin. - You must go to school.
    Girmemeliler. - They mustn't enter.
  -meli is a common way to give advice or make a strong suggestion:
  Doktora gitmelisiniz. (You must see a doctor.)
- Olmak in idiomatic phrases: beyond "to be/become," olmak appears in
  many fixed expressions worth learning as-is:
    oldu - that's settled, that's fine        olur mu? - is that OK?
    ne oluyor? - what's happening?            olamaz - it's not possible
    olsun - let it be, forget it              olabilir - it's possible
- The noun-building ending -lik: attach to adjectives, adverbs, or nouns
  to create new nouns, similar to English "-ness":
    mutlu (happy) -> mutluluk (happiness)     hasta (ill) -> hastalık
    (illness)
  It also creates nouns for objects/places (kimlik - identity card, from
  kim - who) and adjectives meaning "for" (iki kişilik - for two people,
  günlük - daily, from gün - day).

EXAMPLES:

  yew-rew-meh-YEE seh-veh-REEM) - I like walking by the sea.
Futbol seyretmekten nefret ediyorum. (foot-BOHL sey-ret-mek-TEN
  nef-RET eh-dee-yoh-room) - I hate watching football.

  lah-ZUHM) - I need to be at work at eight.

  a doctor.

NOTES:
Notice how Turkish repeats a word for emphasis rather than using an
intensifying adverb — çok çok çalışıyorum ("I work very very much") is a
completely normal and common way to stress a point, not childish or
incorrect. Also, when this unit's speaker uses lazım, note that it's an
Arabic loanword pronounced with a lengthened first vowel (LAA-zuhm) — a
small exception to Turkish's normal short-vowel rule, flagged back in
Lesson 2.

PRACTICE:
1. Say "learning Turkish is easy" using the full infinitive.
2. Turn pişirmek (to cook) into a short-infinitive noun and use it in
   "He stopped cooking."
3. Express "I need to go to work" three different ways: with lazım, with
   gerek, and with -meli.
4. Build a noun meaning "happiness" from mutlu using -lik.
5. What's the difference in meaning (and stress) between İÇ-me suyu! and
   iç-ME suyu?

LESSON 13: If It Doesn't Get Better... — The Present Conditional and Needs
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson introduces the present conditional ("if" sentences about
possible future events), the versatile word kadar ("as...as," "until"),
how to say "since" and "for" when talking about time, how to express
needing something, and the vocabulary for parts of the body.

GRAMMAR:
- Present tense "if" forms of "to be": spot them by -se- inserted into
  the personal endings.
    iyiysem - if I'm well          Türkse - if she's Turkish
    iyiysek - if we're fine        Türkseler (or Türklerse) - if they're
    Turkish
  These same endings attach directly onto verbs to create the if-form of
  the -r present tense: take the -r present form, strip off the personal
  ending, and add the if-ending instead.
    yaparım -> yaparsam - if I do          isterse - if she wants
    açarlar -> açarlarsa - if they open
  Full example: Telefon etmezsem saat ikide buluşacağız. (If I don't
  phone, we'll meet at two.) The word eğer ("if") can optionally be
  placed before the clause for extra clarity, but it's usually not needed
  since the -se ending already signals "if" on its own.
  You can also attach -se directly to var/yok: Paramız yoksa gidemeyiz.
  (If we've got no money, we can't go.)
- Using the -r present tense in the result clause: after an "if" clause,
  Turkish typically follows with the -r present tense (not -ecek) for
  what will happen: Eğer gelirse memnun olurum. (If he comes, I'll be
  happy.) You'll also hear the -r present used alone as an implied
  warning, with the "if" part left unspoken: Düşersin! (You'll fall! —
  meaning "if you're not careful, you'll fall.")
- Needing something — -e ihtiyaç var: the Turkish equivalent of "I need a
  holiday" is literally "I have a need to a holiday."
    Tatile ihtiyacım var. - I need a holiday.
    Neye ihtiyacımız var? - What do we need?
  Note ihtiyaç changes its final ç to c before a vowel-starting ending
  (ihtiyacım, not "ihtiyaçım") — this is the same voiced/voiceless
  consonant harmony pattern from Lesson 2.
- Since — -den beri: for time, "since" is expressed with -den beri.
    Ne zamandan beri? - Since when?         Dünden beri. - Since
    yesterday.
  Note the tense mismatch with English: Turkish says Bu sabahtan beri
  hastayım (literally "I am ill since this morning") where English says
  "I've BEEN ill since this morning" — don't try to translate the English
  perfect tense directly; just use the Turkish present/simple form with
  -den beri.
- For (duration in the past): use -den beri or -dir if the action is
  still ongoing; use nothing extra if the action is already finished.
    Bir haftadan beri burada kalıyorlar. - They've been staying here for
    a week (still ongoing).
    İki gün yemedik. - We didn't eat for two days (finished).
- Kadar: as "until/as far as/up to," it's used as -e kadar:
    Beşe kadar beklerim. - I'll wait until 5.
    Işıklara kadar gidin. - Go as far as the lights.
  On its own, kadar also means "as...as": Senin kadar akıllı değil. (He's
  not as clever as you.) Note that personal pronouns take a possessor
  ending before kadar (bizim kadar - as much as us), but o, onlar, and
  ordinary nouns do not (David kadar - as much as David).
- Parts of the body: a practical vocabulary set, useful well beyond the
  doctor's office — see VOCABULARY below.

  gewn-DEH durt keh-REH ah-lah-bee-LEER) - If necessary, he/she can take
  it four times a day.

  geh-LEEN) - If it isn't better tomorrow, come again.

  holiday.

NOTES:
Loanwords like ishal (diarrhoea) and saat (hour) came into Turkish from
Arabic and can behave slightly irregularly for vowel harmony, because
vowel harmony follows how the word is actually pronounced rather than how
it's spelled — so saatler (hours) rather than the "expected" saatlar. As
with lazım in the previous lesson, these are best treated as memorized
exceptions rather than rule-breakers.

PRACTICE:

3. Say "I need a car" using -e ihtiyaç var.
4. Say "since yesterday" and "for two days" (assume the two days are
   already finished).
5. Name five parts of the body in Turkish.

LESSON 14: You Said It Was Very Quiet! — The -miş Reported Past
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson introduces one of Turkish's most distinctive grammatical
features: the -miş ending, used to report what someone else said, to
signal you're not fully certain about something, to react to something
you've just discovered, or to tell a story or joke. You'll also learn a
cluster of endings for connecting clauses ("when," "after," "before,"
"while").

GRAMMAR:
- The -miş ending: means "apparently," "supposedly," or "reportedly."
  Build it by adding -miş (four-way harmony) to the stem, then the
  personal ending.
    Ben yapmışım. - I did it, apparently (i.e., so I'm told).
    Bitmiş diyorlar. - They say it's finished.
    Arabamı siz sürmüşsünüz. - Apparently it was you who drove my car.
  You can also attach -miş to the verb "to be": İngilizmiş (he is/was
  said to be English), şişmanmışız (they say we are/were fat) — note the
  buffer -y- needed after a vowel-ending word.
- Using -miş: this ending is extremely common in spoken Turkish, and its
  common thread is distancing the speaker from the statement in some
  way. Specific uses include:
    reporting what someone else said: Otel güzelmiş. - They said the
    hotel was nice.
    checking something you're unsure of: Doğru mu yapmışım? - Have I
    done it right?
    reacting to something you've just noticed: Aa! Çorabım kaçmış! - Oh!
    My stocking's laddered!
    telling stories, jokes, or fairy tales: Bir güzel prenses varmış... -
    There was a beautiful princess... (this is the Turkish equivalent of
    "once upon a time")
- -miştir/-mişlerdir: these forms carry two special meanings beyond the
  basic -miş sense.
    "I expect...": used when you're not sure something's happened but
    assume it has. Şimdi oraya gelmiştir. - She must have arrived by
    now.
    the "journalistic" official past: used in announcements and news
    reports as a formal-sounding equivalent of the simple past tense —
    Ankara treni üçüncü perona gelmiştir. (The Ankara train has arrived
    at platform three.)
- Nouns and adjectives that simply end in -miş as part of their basic
  form (not the grammatical ending): geçmiş (the past), dolmuş (a
  "stuffed" shared taxi — literally "having become full"), kızarmış
  (fried). These are worth recognizing so you don't mistake them for the
  reported-past ending on a completely different root.
- Clause-connecting endings: attach directly to verb stems (or, for
  -dikten sonra and -ken, to specific tense forms) to link two actions.
    -ince - on...-ing, when: İstanbul'a gelince otelimize gittik. - On
    arriving in İstanbul, we went to our hotel.
    -dikten sonra - after...-ing: Yemeğini yedikten sonra uyudu. - She
    fell asleep after eating her meal.
    -ken (added to the -r present "he/she" form) - whilst...-ing:
    Temizlerken kafamı çarptım. - Whilst cleaning I banged my head.
    -den önce (added to the short infinitive) - before...-ing: Alışveriş
    yapmadan önce kütüphaneye gitti. - Before doing the shopping, he
    went to the library.

  loh-kahn-tah-SUH bee-reen-JEE suh-nuhf-MUHSH) - According to you, the
  hotel's restaurant was supposed to be first class.

  was a beautiful princess... (once upon a time...)

  oh-teh-lee-mee-ZEH geet-TEEK) - On arriving in İstanbul, we went to
  our hotel.

  oo-yoo-DOO) - She fell asleep after eating her meal.
Şimdi oraya gelmiştir. (sheem-DEE oh-rah-YAH gel-meesh-TEER) - She must
  have arrived there by now.

NOTES:
The -miş tense is sometimes jokingly called the "gossip tense" in
descriptions of Turkish, because it lets the speaker report something
while quietly signaling "I wasn't there, but..." It's worth actively
listening for in real Turkish speech and media — once you notice it,
you'll hear it constantly, especially in storytelling and casual
conversation about other people's business.

LESSON 15: Wrong Number — Participles: -en vs. -dik vs. -ecek
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson completes the set of Turkish participles (verb-based
adjectives), teaching you -dik adjectives for the past/present object of
an action and -ecek adjectives for the future object, and clarifies
exactly how they differ from the -en adjectives you met in Lesson 10.
You'll also learn the -ip ending for chaining actions together and how to
intensify adjectives using a special prefix pattern.

GRAMMAR:
- -dik adjectives (the "object participle"): describe the OBJECT of an
  action — the thing being acted upon — where -en (Lesson 10) describes
  the SUBJECT (the doer). Build with stem + -dik (four-way harmony) +
  possessed ending.
    (benim) sevdiğim renk - the colour which I like (or liked)
    (onun) sürdüğü araba - the car he drives (or drove)
  -dik adjectives are flexible about which relationship they express,
  depending on context:
    plain object (which/that): sevdiğim renk - the colour I like
    location (in/at which): oturduğum ev - the house in which I live
    destination (to which): gittiği okul - the school he goes to
    source (from which): geldiğiniz köy - the village you come from
    companion (with which/whom): evlendiği erkek - the man she married
  Note that -dik adjectives can refer to either the past or present — the
  meaning is usually clear from context.
- The crucial distinction between -en and -dik: use -en for the SUBJECT
  of the action (who's doing it), and -dik for the OBJECT (who/what it's
  being done to).
    Beni seven kadın. - The woman who loves me. (she is the subject)
    Sevdiğim kadın. - The woman whom I love. (she is the object)
- -dik as a noun (dropping the described noun): when it's obvious what
  the missing noun is, you can drop it and move its ending onto the -dik
  form itself.
    Aldığım şeyi istiyor musun? -> Aldığımı istiyor musun? - Do you want
    the thing I bought? -> Do you want what I bought?
  If the missing noun was plural, carry the plural ending over too:
  Aldığım şeyleri -> Aldıklarımı.
- -ecek adjectives and nouns: the future counterpart of -dik, used for
  "which/that I will..." Build with stem + -ecek (two-way harmony) +
  possessed ending.
    Okuyacağım kitap. - The book which I'm going to read.
  Like -dik, -ecek forms can also stand alone as nouns: Alacaklarım
  bunlar. (These are the ones I'm going to buy.) And like -en/-dik,
  -ecek covers both subject and object roles depending on context: Yapan
  bu (this is the one who did it) vs. Yapacak bu (this is the one who'll
  do it); Yaptığı bu (this is the one he did) vs. Yapacağı bu (this is
  the one he'll do).
- The -ip ending: lets you chain two actions together with the same
  subject and matching tense, dropping the repeated tense marker on the
  first verb — similar to how English can say "I'm going to have a bath
  and wash my hair" instead of repeating "I'm going to."
    Bu akşam banyo yapacağım ve saçlarımı yıkayacağım. -> Bu akşam banyo
    yapıp saçlarımı yıkayacağım.
  You CANNOT use -ip if the verb forms differ, or if the two actions have
  different subjects: Eve döneceğiz ve çalışacağım (We'll go home and
  I'll work) cannot be shortened this way, because "we" and "I" are
  different subjects.
- Intensifying adjectives with a repeated prefix: Turkish has one case
  where it adds a PREFIX instead of a suffix — to intensify certain
  adjectives, repeat the adjective's first syllable-initial sound plus
  a fixed consonant (usually p, m, or s), then attach it to the front of
  the full word.
    kırmızı (red) -> kıpkırmızı (bright red)
    beyaz (white) -> bembeyaz (white as snow)
    temiz (clean) -> tertemiz (squeaky clean)
    boş (empty) -> bomboş (completely empty)
  There's no single rule for which consonant to use — it's best learned
  by encountering each intensified form as a fixed expression.

speakers, since English "which/who" doesn't mark this subject/object
difference at all. A reliable trick: ask yourself who or what is
performing the action versus who or what is receiving it — the performer
gets -en, the receiver gets -dik.
