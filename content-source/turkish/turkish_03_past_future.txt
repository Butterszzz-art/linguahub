LESSON 8: Have You Been Waiting Long? — The Past Tense and Telling Time
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson introduces the simple past tense — the tense you use for
anything that happened and finished, including situations where English
would use "have done." You'll also learn to tell the time on the hour and
half-hour, build compound nouns, and recognize verbs that insist on a
particular case ending for their object.

GRAMMAR:
- The past tense of "to be": spot it by the ending -di (two-way harmony,
  so also -dı/-du/-dü). zenginim (I am rich) -> zengindim (I was rich).
  If the word ends in a vowel, insert a buffer -y-: İyiydim (I was good).
  Negate with değil plus these same endings: Mutlu değildim (I wasn't
  happy).
- The simple past tense of verbs: take the stem, then add the past "to
  be" ending directly.
    geldim - I came        güldün - you smiled       seyretti - he watched
    gittik - we went       başladınız - you started   beklediler - they
    waited
  Negate by inserting -me- before the past ending: gelmedim (I didn't
  come). Form questions by adding mı/mi/mu/mü AFTER the personal ending
  (note this is different from the -iyor tense, where the question
  particle splits the verb and the "to be" part): geldim mi? (did I
  come?), gelmedim mi? (didn't I come?)
- Using the past tense for "have done": Turkish has no direct equivalent
  of the English "have done" tense — you simply use the simple past
  instead.
    Hiç rakı içtin mi? - Have you ever drunk rakı?
    Türkiye'ye gitmedim. - I haven't been to Turkey.
  Conversely, Turks often use the PAST tense in conversation where English
  would use the present: Geç kaldım (literally "I was late") is used to
  mean "I'm late"; Anladım ("I understood") means "I understand";
  Geldim! ("I came!") means "I'm coming! I'll be right there!"
- Telling the time (hours and half-hours):
    Saat kaç? - What's the time?          Saat üç. - It's three o'clock.
    Saat kaçta? - At what time?           Saat üçte. - At three o'clock.
    Saat dört buçuk. - It's half past four.
  One oddity: half past twelve is simply yarım, not "on iki buçuk."
- Compound nouns: when you describe one noun with another (like "cheese
  sandwich" in English), the second noun takes the possessed ending -i/-si
  you learned in Lesson 7.
    pizza evi - pizza house       yatak odası - bedroom
    Atatürk Havalimanı - Atatürk Airport
  Remember su (water) is irregular, taking -yu: portakal suyu (orange
  juice), maden suyu (mineral water).
- Verbs that need -e or -den instead of -i: not every verb's object takes
  the direct-object -i ending. Some verbs — including bakmak (to look at),
  aşık olmak (to fall in love with), and binmek (to get into/onto) —
  insist their object take -e instead:
    Melek arabaya baktı. - Melek looked at the car.
  Others — including bıkmak (to get fed up with), inmek (to get out of),
  and nefret etmek (to hate) — insist on -den:
    Melek arabadan indi. - Melek got out of the car.
  There's no rule to predict which verbs behave this way; you simply learn
  each one's required ending as you meet it (dictionaries mark them, e.g.
  binmek (-e)).
- İçin ("for," "for the sake of," "because of"): when için refers to a
  personal pronoun, give the pronoun a possessor ending first: benim için
  (for me), Senin için aldım (I bought it for you). When it refers to a
  plain noun, no ending is needed: Bu bira Ahmet için (This beer is for
  Ahmet). An alternative way to say "for" is simply the -e ending: Bu
  bira Ahmet'e (same meaning).
- Question word combinations: you can build extra question words by
  adding endings or için to ne (what) or kim (who): neden/niçin/niye
  (why?), nerede (where?), neyin (of what?), kimin (whose?), kim için
  (who for?).
- Word order with mı and de: place mı or de (meaning "also") directly
  after the specific word they refer to, since where you put them changes
  the meaning: Bana bira getiriyor musun? (Are you getting me a beer?) vs.
  Bana bira mı getiriyorsun? (Is it BEER that you're getting me?)

simply teşekkür ederim ("thank you") — there's no need to add "fine" or
"well" the way English does. Also note how iyiydim inserts a buffer -y-
despite there being no actual vowel clash — this is simply a fixed feature
of the "to be" past tense after a vowel-ending word, worth memorizing as
an exception.

PRACTICE:

2. Make a past-tense question: "Did you wait?" and "Didn't you wait?"
3. Say "half past four" and "half past twelve" in Turkish.
4. Build a compound noun for "orange juice."
5. Which case ending does the object of bakmak (to look at) require —
   -i, -e, or -den?

LESSON 9: I'll Get Up Early Tomorrow — The Future Tense and Place Words
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers the "real" future tense (as opposed to using -iyor with
future meaning, which you already know from Lesson 7), how to make casual
suggestions, the word ile ("with"/"and"), how to say where something is in
relation to something else (on top of, behind, between, etc.), and how to
tell the time more precisely than just the hour and half-hour.

GRAMMAR:
- The future tense: spot it by -ecek (two-way harmony: -ecek/-acak). Build
  it the same way as other tenses: stem + -ecek + personal ending.
    geleceğim - I will come      satacaksın - you will sell
    yazacağız - we will write    bekleyecekler - they will wait
  Negate by inserting -me- before -ecek, with a buffer -y- always required
  between them: geleceğim -> gelmeyeceğim (I won't come). Form questions
  by splitting the verb from its personal ending and inserting mı/mi/mu/mü
  between them, same pattern as the past tense; the "they" form again puts
  mı at the very end.
  You can also use olmak (to become/be) as the future of "to be," var, or
  yok: Mutluyum -> Mutlu olacağım (I will be happy); Su yok -> Su
  olmayacak (There won't be any water).
- The present continuous (-iyor) can ALSO carry future meaning, and
  Turkish speakers switch freely between it and -ecek — often the two are
  interchangeable, much like English "I'm coming tomorrow" vs. "I will
  come tomorrow."
- Informal suggestions with -sene/-senize: add this ending to a verb stem
  to mean "why don't you...!" Gelsene! (Do come!) Baksana! (Why don't you
  look!) Depending on tone of voice, these can be either a friendly nudge
  or an expression of mild impatience.
- Ile ("with," and sometimes "and"): can be shortened to the ending -le
  (two-way harmony).
    Kardeşimle git. - Go with my brother.
    Arabayla gidiyorum. - I'm going by car.
  Turkish also uses ile in some places English uses "and": Pınar ile
  Didem geliyorlar (Pınar and Didem are coming). With personal pronouns,
  -le attaches to the possessive form (except for "they"): Benimle gel.
  (Come with me.) Note the difference between -le (with/and, from ile)
  and -li (an adjective-forming ending, e.g. sütlü, "with milk," meyveli,
  "with fruit") — they look similar but serve different jobs.
- Prepositions of place: Turkish builds these from nouns meaning the
  relevant body part or zone, combined with a possessive and the -de/-e/
  -den family of endings.
    üst (top) -> -in üstünde (on top of)
    alt (bottom) -> -in altında (under)
    ön (front) -> -in önünde (in front of)
    arka (back) -> -in arkasında (behind)
    yan (side) -> -in yanında (beside)
    orta (middle) -> -in ortasında (in the middle of)
    iç (interior) -> -in içinde (inside)
    dış (exterior) -> -in dışında (outside)
    ara (gap) -> -in arasında (between)
    karşı (opposite) -> -in karşısında (opposite)
  Examples: Masanın altından geldi. (The cat came from under the table.)
  PTT'nin karşısında. (Opposite the post office.) Otel ile plajın
  arasında. (Between the hotel and the beach.)
- Telling the time accurately: for minutes before the hour, use -e var
  ("there are ___ to"); for minutes after the hour, use -i geçiyor
  ("___ is passing"). To express a specific moment ("at five to," "at
  five past") swap var for kala and geçiyor for geçe.
    Saat üçe on var. - It's ten to three.
    Saat üçü on geçiyor. - It's ten past three.
    Saat bire beş kala. - At five to one.
    Saat biri beş geçe. - At five past one.

  beel-mee-yoh-ROOM) - What are you doing this evening? I don't know.
Yarın erken kalkacağım. (yah-RUHN ehr-KEN kahl-kah-jah-UHM) - I'll get up
  early tomorrow.

  gee-dee-yoh-ROOM) - Go with my brother. I'm going by car.

  kahr-shuh-suhn-DAH. oh-TEL ee-LEH plah-JUHN ah-rah-suhn-DAH) - It's
  opposite the post office. Between the hotel and the beach.

  four.

NOTES:
In this unit's dialogue, notice how the speakers move fluidly between
-iyor and -ecek to talk about the same future evening — this is completely
normal and not a mistake in either direction. Also, hadi (come on!) is a
word you'll hear constantly in Turkey; try counting how many times you
hear it in a single day once you're there.

PRACTICE:

2. Say "I might come with you" using -sene as an informal suggestion form
   applied to gel-.
3. Translate: "The cat is under the table" and "in front of the grocer's."
4. Give the time for "twenty-five to twelve" and "twenty past six."
5. Explain the difference between -le (from ile) and -li in your own
   words, with an example of each.

LESSON 10: Would You Help Me? — The -r Present Tense and -en Adjectives
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson introduces Turkish's SECOND present tense — traditionally
called the "wide" or aorist present — which is used very differently from
-iyor: for promises, willingness, requests, habitual truths, and
storytelling. You'll also learn to build adjectives out of verbs (the
equivalent of English "-ing" adjectives like "the running man"), the
"by...-ing" ending -erek, and more verbs that require a specific case
ending on their object.

GRAMMAR:
- The -r present tense: build it by adding -r, -ir, or -er to the verb
  stem (plus the personal "to be" ending). Which one you use depends on
  the stem:
    - stems ending in a vowel: just add -r.        söylerim (I say)
    - one-syllable stems (most): add -er.          geçerim (I pass)
    - one-syllable stems ending in -r or -l: add -ir. kalırım (I stay)
    - stems with more than one syllable: add -ir (four-way harmony).
      inanırım (I believe)
  There's no fully reliable rule for which one-syllable verbs take -er
  vs. -ir — these are best learned by encountering each verb individually.
- Negative -r present forms: spot them by -mez/-maz. Build by adding -me
  to the stem, then -z (except for the "I" and "we" forms, which drop the
  z in statements but restore it in questions).
    almam - I don't take          bilmezsin - you don't know
    bulmayız - we don't find      bulmaz mıyız? - don't we find?
- Uses of the -r present tense: this tense covers a cluster of related
  meanings quite different from -iyor:
    making promises: Sana dondurma alırım. - I'll buy you an ice-cream.
    willingness: Yardım ederim. - I'll help.
    intention: Yarın gelirim. - I'll come tomorrow.
    polite requests: Kapıyı açar mısınız? - Would you open the door?
    offers: Çay içer misiniz? - Would you like some tea?
    set phrases: Teşekkür ederim. - Thank you.
    telling stories/jokes: Üç erkek bara girerler... - Three men go into
      a bar...
    unchanging facts: Su 0°C altında donar. - Water freezes below 0°C.
    habitual actions: Sık sık plaja giderim. - I often go to the beach.
    uncertainty/hope: Belki gelir. - Maybe he'll come. Umarım,
      gitmezsiniz. - I hope you won't go.
- -en adjectives (the "subject participle"): turn a verb into an adjective
  describing whoever performs the action, similar to English "-ing"
  adjectives. Add -en (two-way harmony) to the stem.
    koşan adam - the running man, the man who's running
    İstanbul'a giden tren - the train going to İstanbul
  You can also use -en adjectives without a noun, in which case they act
  like nouns meaning "the one(s) who...": Masada oturan güzeldi. (The one
  sitting at the table was pretty.) Add -ler to make them plural.
- -erek ("by...-ing"): attach to a verb stem to describe how something was
  done.
    Yürüyerek geldim. - I came on foot (by walking).
    Gazeteye bakarak buldum. - I found it by looking at the newspaper.
  It can also just mean "-ing": Gülerek gitti. (She left smiling.) The
  -erek form of olmak is special and means "as": Programcı olarak
  çalışıyorum. (I work as a programmer.)
- More verbs needing -e, -den, or ile instead of -i: continuing from
  Lesson 8, here are more examples to build your instinct for these:
    -e: sormak (to ask), yardım etmek (to help), vurmak (to hit)
    -den: hoşlanmak (to enjoy), korkmak (to fear), bahsetmek (to mention)
    ile: evlenmek (to marry) — Bir elektrikçiyle evlenecek. (She's going
      to marry an electrician.)

wonder," "by any chance") is a small word that makes a request noticeably
more polite — sprinkle it into requests when you want to soften them.

LESSON 11: Where Shall We Go? What Can We Do? — Ability, Comparatives, and "Let's"
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
This lesson covers how to say "can/can't" and "might/might not," how to
compare two things, the full set of "let" forms (let me, let's, let him),
the useful adjective-forming ending -ki, and a tidy review of when to use
each buffer consonant.

GRAMMAR:
- Can/be able to: -ebil- (four-way, e-type harmony) slots in right after
  the verb stem, inside any tense.
    gidebilirim - I can go        yapabilirsin - you can do
    gidebileceğim - I'll be able to go   Oynayabildi mi? - Was he able to
    play?
  Form questions the normal way for whichever tense you're in:
  gidebilir miyim? (can I go?)
- Can't/not able: mark with -e- (note: NOT -ebil-, just -e-) right after
  the stem, in the negative form of whichever tense you need.
    gelemeyeceğim - I won't be able to come
    Yapamadım. - I couldn't do it.
    Olamaz! - It can't be!
- Might: also uses -ebil-, with the specific meaning (can vs. might)
  usually clear from context.
    Biraz sonra içebilirim. - I might have a drink a little later.
  The negative of "might" is different from the negative of "can" — for
  "might not," simply add -me after the verb stem (not -e-):
    Yapabilirim, yapmayabilirim. - I might do it and I might not.
  Compare: Sinemaya gidemem (I can't go to the cinema) vs. Sinemaya
  gitmeyebilirim (I might not go to the cinema) — these mean different
  things, so don't mix them up.
- Comparatives: to say "more X than Y," Turkish literally says "more X
  FROM Y" — the compared-against item takes the -den ending, and daha
  ("more") appears before the adjective.
    Senden daha akıllıyım. - I'm cleverer than you (literally "from you
    more clever am-I").
    Türkiye, İngiltere'den daha ucuz. - Turkey is cheaper than England.
  For superlatives ("the most / the ...-est"), use en before the
  adjective: Türkiye'de en iyi şarkıcı Pınar. (Pınar is the best singer
  in Turkey.)
- The "let" forms of verbs:
    -eyim - "let me..." (offering help, making suggestions):
      Yardım edeyim. - Let me help. Gideyim mi? - Shall I go?
    -elim - "let's..." (making suggestions): Gidelim. - Let's go.
      Yapalım mı? - Shall we do it?
    -sin - what someone ELSE should do, or a hope that something happens:
      Eve gitsin. - He should go home. Yağmur yağmasın. - I hope it
      doesn't rain (literally "let it not rain").
    -sinler - the plural of -sin: Yarın gelsinler. - Tell them to come
      tomorrow.
  You'll recognize several fixed phrases built this way: Üstü kalsın.
  (Keep the change.) Kolay gelsin. (I hope your work goes well.) Afiyet
  olsun. (Bon appétit.)
- The ending -ki: attach to a noun already carrying -de to turn it into
  an adjective meaning "the one which is in/at/on."
    otel + de -> oteldeki bar - the bar in the hotel
    masa + da -> masadaki çiçekler - the flowers on the table
  -ki never changes for vowel harmony. Used without its noun, it means
  "the one(s) which is/are in/at/on": oteldeki (the one in the hotel).
  You can also attach -ki to a possessor to mean "the one which is
  mine/his/etc.": benimki (mine), onlarınki (theirs).
- Buffer consonants, reviewed and organized: now that you've met several
  endings, here's the full pattern for which buffer to use:
    -s- : for possessed endings, and in compound nouns
    -n- : for possessor endings; after a word that already has a
      possessed ending; with bu/şu/o; with the -ki ending
    -y- : everywhere else a vowel clash needs to be avoided
- -ce ("according to," and to name a language): add to a nationality or
  country name to give the related language (Türk -> Türkçe; İsveç ->
  İsveççe). Add to a personal pronoun to mean "according to...": bence
  (in my opinion), sizce (in your opinion). Add to an adjective to mean
  "rather...": iyice (rather good), büyükçe (rather big).

NOTES:
Comparative sentences reverse English logic — instead of saying "I'm
cleverer THAN you," Turkish says "FROM you more clever" — so make sure
the -den ending lands on the thing being compared against, not the person
doing the comparing. Also, exclamations like üf be! and yok canım! are
common and hard to translate literally, but their emotional tone usually
comes through clearly from context and delivery.

PRACTICE:
1. Say "I can come" and then "I can't come" for gelmek.
2. Explain the difference between Sinemaya gidemem and Sinemaya
   gitmeyebilirim.
3. Say "Turkey is cheaper than England" using the comparative pattern.
4. Give the "let's" form of gitmek and the "let him" form of gelmek.
5. Build oteldeki using -ki, and explain what it means.
