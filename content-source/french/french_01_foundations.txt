LESSON 1: The French Alphabet & Pronunciation Basics
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
French uses the same 26 letters as English, but many are pronounced very
differently, and several sounds don't exist in English at all. This lesson
builds the sound system you'll rely on for everything else.

GRAMMAR:
- The alphabet (l'alphabet) is recited: a (ah), b (bay), c (say), d (day),
  e (euh), f (eff), g (zhay), h (ash), i (ee), j (zhee), k (kah), l (ell),
  m (emm), n (enn), o (oh), p (pay), q (kew), r (air), s (ess), t (tay),
  u (ew), v (vay), w (doobluh-vay), x (eeks), y (ee-grek), z (zed).
- Vowel sounds: French vowels are pure and held steady (no gliding like
  English "day" -> "day-ee"). Key sounds:
    a  -> "ah"        (as in "papa")
    e  -> "uh"         (as in "le")
    é  -> "ay"         (as in "café")
    è/ê -> "eh"        (as in "père")
    i  -> "ee"         (as in "ici")
    o  -> "oh"         (as in "mot")
    u  -> pursed "ew" — round your lips as if for "oo" but say "ee"
          (this sound doesn't exist in English; contrast tu/tout)
    ou -> "oo"         (as in "vous")
    oi -> "wah"        (as in "moi")
    eu -> "uh" (rounded)(as in "peu")
- Nasal vowels (air pushed through the nose, N/M not fully pronounced):
    an/am/en/em -> nasal "ah"   (as in "dans", "temps")
    on/om       -> nasal "oh"   (as in "bon")
    in/im/ain/ein -> nasal "eh" (as in "vin", "pain")
    un/um       -> nasal "uh"   (as in "un" — merging with "in" in much
                    of modern spoken French)
- Consonants mostly match English, with exceptions:
    c  -> "s" before e/i/y, "k" elsewhere (cinéma vs. cadeau)
    ç  -> always "s" (garçon)
    g  -> "zh" before e/i/y, hard "g" elsewhere (genou vs. gâteau)
    j  -> always "zh" (jour)
    h  -> always silent, but see Lesson 2 for mute vs. aspirate h
    r  -> a soft guttural sound from the back of the throat (not the
          American r) — practice with "rouge", "Paris"
    ll -> usually "y" after i (fille = "fee-yuh"), but "l" elsewhere
    qu -> "k" (qui = "kee")
    th -> "t" (théâtre)
- Final consonants are usually SILENT: petit -> "puh-tee" (t silent).
  Common exceptions: c, r, f, l are often pronounced (the mnemonic "CaReFuL"
  helps you remember which final consonants tend to be sounded).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(not applicable — this lesson covers sounds, not verbs)

VOCABULARY:
bonjour - hello / good day
merci - thank you
oui - yes
non - no
s'il vous plaît - please (formal)
excusez-moi - excuse me
au revoir - goodbye

EXAMPLES:
Je m'appelle Marie. - My name is Marie. (practice the nasal "en" in "appelle")
Il fait beau. - The weather is nice. (practice silent final -t, nasal "ai")
Nous allons au marché. - We're going to the market. (practice "ou", nasal "on")

NOTES:
- The single most common beginner mistake is pronouncing final consonants
  the way English does. When in doubt, drop the final letter unless it's
  c, r, f, or l.
- The French "u" (tu, une, du) is the hardest sound for English speakers —
  round your lips tightly and try to say "ee" without moving your jaw.

PRACTICE:
Say these pairs aloud and notice the difference: tu / tout, dessous / dessus,
bon / banc, vin / vent.

LESSON 2: Accents, Liaison, and Elision
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
French uses five accent marks that change pronunciation or meaning, plus two
"linking" rules — liaison and elision — that make spoken French flow
smoothly between words.

GRAMMAR:
- The five accents:
    é  accent aigu    (acute)   -> closed "ay" sound (café, étudiant)
    è  accent grave    (grave)   -> open "eh" sound (père, très)
    à/ù accent grave    (grave)   -> no sound change, distinguishes words
        (a "has" vs. à "to/at"; ou "or" vs. où "where")
    ê  accent circonflexe (circumflex) -> often marks a dropped historical
        "s" (hôpital = hospital, forêt = forest) and slightly opens the vowel
    ë/ï accent tréma (diaeresis) -> forces two vowels to be pronounced
        separately (Noël = "no-el", not "nwel")
    ç  cédille (cedilla) -> forces a soft "s" sound on c before a, o, u
        (garçon, français)
- Liaison: when a word ending in a normally-silent consonant is followed by
  a word starting with a vowel or mute h, that consonant IS pronounced,
  linking the two words. Example: vous_avez ("voo-zah-vay", not "voo ah-vay");
  les_amis ("lay-zah-mee"); un petit_ami ("uh puh-tee-tah-mee").
  Liaison is mandatory after: subject pronouns before verbs, articles before
  nouns, and short adjectives before nouns. It's forbidden after "et" (and).
- Elision: when a short word (je, me, te, se, le, la, de, ne, que, ce, si+il)
  ends in a vowel and the next word starts with a vowel or mute h, the vowel
  is dropped and replaced with an apostrophe. je + aime -> j'aime;
  le + école is impossible because école is feminine (l'école); si + il ->
  s'il.

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(not applicable)

VOCABULARY:
l'hôtel - the hotel (mute h, takes liaison/elision)
le héros - the hero (aspirate h, blocks liaison/elision)
un ami / une amie - a friend (m/f)
les enfants - the children
c'est - it is / that is

EXAMPLES:
J'habite à Paris. - I live in Paris. (elision: je -> j')
Vous êtes français ? - Are you French? (liaison: vous_êtes)
Les hôtels sont chers. - The hotels are expensive. (liaison, mute h)
Le héros arrive. - The hero arrives. (NO liaison — aspirate h)

NOTES:
- Most words starting with "h" in French have a MUTE h and behave like
  vowels for elision/liaison purposes. A small set (héros, haricot, honte,
  hall, etc.) have an ASPIRATE h, historically pronounced, which now blocks
  both elision and liaison even though the h itself stays silent. Dictionaries
  mark aspirate-h words with an asterisk.
- Common learner mistake: adding liaison where it's forbidden (after "et")
  or forgetting it where it's mandatory (after plural articles: les_amis).

PRACTICE:
Read aloud: "Nous_avons deux_enfants et une amie." Notice liaison after
"nous" and "deux" but not after "et".

LESSON 3: Key Parts of Speech — The Big Picture
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Before diving into verb tenses and pronoun rules, it helps to see the whole
map: what a noun, article, pronoun, adjective, and adverb do in a French
sentence, and how French sentence order compares to English.

GRAMMAR:
- Nouns (les noms) always have a gender (masculine or feminine) in French,
  even for objects with no natural gender (la table = the table, f.; le
  livre = the book, m.). Gender is not predictable from meaning and must
  largely be memorized with the noun.
- Articles (les articles) agree in gender and number with the noun they
  introduce: le/la/les (the), un/une/des (a/an/some), du/de la/des
  (some, partitive). Covered in depth in Lesson 4 and Part 6.
- Subject pronouns (je, tu, il/elle/on, nous, vous, ils/elles) replace a
  noun as the subject of a verb. Note "tu" (informal "you", one person) vs.
  "vous" (formal "you" singular, OR plural "you" — always).
- Adjectives (les adjectifs) agree in gender and number with the noun they
  describe, and in French MOST adjectives come AFTER the noun (une voiture
  rouge = a red car), unlike English. A short list of common adjectives
  (beauty, age, goodness, size — remembered by the acronym BAGS) come
  BEFORE the noun (une belle voiture = a beautiful car).
- Adverbs (les adverbes) modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs and are
  usually invariable (they don't change form). Many are formed by adding
  -ment to the feminine form of an adjective, similar to English "-ly"
  (lente -> lentement = slowly).
- Basic word order: Subject + Verb + Object, same as English:
  Je mange une pomme. (I eat an apple.) The big structural differences
  come with object pronouns (which move BEFORE the verb — see Part 6) and
  adjective placement (see above).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Subject pronouns:
je (j') - I
tu - you (informal, singular)
il / elle / on - he / she / one (also "we" informally)
nous - we
vous - you (formal singular, or plural)
ils / elles - they (m./m.+mixed / f.)

VOCABULARY:
un nom - a noun
un verbe - a verb
un adjectif - an adjective
un adverbe - an adverb
une phrase - a sentence
la grammaire - grammar

EXAMPLES:
Le petit chat noir dort. - The little black cat is sleeping.
  (petit = BAGS adjective, before noun; noir = color, after noun)
Elle parle lentement. - She speaks slowly. (adverb from adjective "lente")
Nous aimons la musique française. - We love French music.

NOTES:
- "On" technically means "one/people in general" but in everyday spoken
  French it's used constantly to mean "we" (with a 3rd-person singular
  verb): On mange ? = Shall we eat?
- Because most adjectives follow the noun, and a handful precede it,
  memorize the BAGS list early: Beauty (beau, joli), Age (jeune, vieux,
  nouveau), Goodness (bon, mauvais), Size (grand, petit, gros).

PRACTICE:
Identify the part of speech of each word in: "Ma petite sœur parle
rapidement." (ma=possessive adj., petite=adjective, sœur=noun,
parle=verb, rapidement=adverb)

LESSON 4: Articles and Nouns — The Basics
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Every French noun needs an article (or another determiner) in front of it
almost always — French rarely drops the article the way English does. This
lesson covers the core system; Part 6 goes deeper into tricky cases.

GRAMMAR:
- Definite articles ("the"): le (m. sing.), la (f. sing.), l' (before a
  vowel/mute h, either gender), les (plural, either gender).
- Indefinite articles ("a/an", "some"): un (m. sing.), une (f. sing.),
  des (plural, either gender).
- Partitive articles ("some/any", for uncountable amounts): du (m. sing.),
  de la (f. sing.), de l' (before vowel/mute h), des (plural). Used for
  food/drink and abstract quantities: du pain (some bread), de la patience
  (some patience).
- After a negation, un/une/des/du/de la generally become simply "de" (or
  "d'" before a vowel): J'ai un chien -> Je n'ai pas de chien.
- Gender clues (not 100% reliable, but useful patterns):
    Often masculine: nouns ending in -age, -ment, -eau, -isme
    Often feminine: nouns ending in -tion, -sion, -té, -ette, -ure
- Plural of nouns: add -s in writing (usually silent in speech) —
  un livre -> des livres. Nouns ending in -eau/-eu often take -x
  (un bateau -> des bateaux). Nouns ending in -s/-x/-z don't change
  (une souris -> des souris).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Article summary:
              masc. sing.   fem. sing.   before vowel/h   plural
definite:     le             la           l'               les
indefinite:   un             une          un / une         des
partitive:    du             de la        de l'            des

VOCABULARY:
le pain - the bread
la pomme - the apple
l'eau (f.) - the water
les fruits - the fruits
un livre - a book
une table - a table
de l'argent - some money

EXAMPLES:
Je voudrais du pain et de l'eau. - I would like some bread and some water.
Elle a une sœur et un frère. - She has a sister and a brother.
Il n'y a pas de lait. - There isn't any milk. (partitive -> "de" after negation)
Les enfants aiment les bonbons. - Children love candy. (definite article used
  for general statements, unlike English)

NOTES:
- Unlike English, French uses the definite article for general/abstract
  statements: "J'aime le café" = "I like coffee" (in general), not "I like
  THE coffee."
- Gender must be learned with each noun — always learn "le/la + noun"
  together as a unit, never the noun alone.

PRACTICE:
Fill in the article: ___ (the) chat mange ___ (some) poisson. (Answer: Le
chat mange du poisson.)

LESSON 5: Numbers
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Numbers come up constantly — prices, ages, phone numbers, dates. French
numbers have a few quirky patterns above 60 that are worth learning early.

GRAMMAR:
- 0–20 must simply be memorized (see vocabulary below); they don't follow a
  clean pattern.
- 21, 31, 41, 51, 61, 71 use "et" before "un": vingt et un (21), trente et
  un (31)... but NOT 81 or 91 (quatre-vingt-un, no "et").
- 22–29, 32–39, etc. are formed with a hyphen: vingt-deux (22).
- 70–79: France uses base-60 counting — soixante-dix (70, literally
  "sixty-ten"), soixante-onze (71), ... soixante-dix-neuf (79).
- 80–89: quatre-vingts (80, literally "four-twenties"), quatre-vingt-un (81,
  no plural -s on "vingt" once followed by another number), ...
  quatre-vingt-dix-neuf (99, "four-twenty-nineteen").
  (Note: Belgium and Switzerland use septante=70, huitante/octante=80,
  nonante=90 — simpler, but not standard in France.)
- 100+ : cent (100), cent un (101, no "et"), deux cents (200, "cent" takes
  -s only when a round multiple with nothing after it — deux cents but
  deux cent un), mille (1,000, NEVER takes -s: deux mille), un million,
  un milliard.
- Ordinal numbers ("first, second..."): add -ième to the cardinal number
  (minus a silent final -e): quatre -> quatrième. Irregular: premier/première
  (1st), and second/seconde is an alternative to deuxième (2nd).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
0 zéro   1 un/une   2 deux   3 trois   4 quatre   5 cinq
6 six    7 sept     8 huit   9 neuf    10 dix
11 onze  12 douze   13 treize  14 quatorze  15 quinze
16 seize 17 dix-sept 18 dix-huit 19 dix-neuf 20 vingt
30 trente  40 quarante  50 cinquante  60 soixante
70 soixante-dix   80 quatre-vingts   90 quatre-vingt-dix
100 cent   1 000 mille   1 000 000 un million

VOCABULARY:
un numéro - a number
un prix - a price
l'âge (m.) - the age
combien - how much/many
gratuit - free (of charge)

EXAMPLES:
J'ai vingt-cinq ans. - I am 25 years old. (French uses "avoir X ans", lit.
  "to have X years", not "to be")
Ça coûte quatre-vingt-quinze euros. - That costs 95 euros.
Elle habite au premier étage. - She lives on the first floor (= 2nd floor
  US-style, since "rez-de-chaussée" is the ground floor).
C'est mon deuxième voyage en France. - This is my second trip to France.

NOTES:
- Age, hunger, thirst, fear, and being right/wrong all use "avoir" (to
  have) in French where English uses "to be": j'ai faim (I'm hungry, lit.
  "I have hunger"), j'ai raison (I'm right).
- Practice 70–99 out loud until the math becomes automatic — it's the #1
  numbers stumbling block for English speakers.

PRACTICE:
Say aloud: 71, 82, 95, 99, 100, 201, 1 000 000.

LESSON 6: Days, Months, Dates, and Seasons
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Talking about dates, the calendar, and seasons — practical vocabulary tied
to the numbers from Lesson 5.

GRAMMAR:
- Days and months are NOT capitalized in French (unlike English): lundi,
  janvier — capitalize only at the start of a sentence.
- To give the date: "le" + cardinal number + month (no "de" between number
  and month, except "premier"): le 3 mai (May 3rd); le premier mai (May 1st
  — this is the ONE ordinal used for dates).
- "On [a day]" needs no preposition: Je travaille lundi. (I work on Monday.)
  Adding "le" before the day makes it a recurring/habitual event:
  Je travaille le lundi. (I work on Mondays / every Monday.)
- Seasons use "en" except spring: en été, en automne, en hiver, BUT
  au printemps (in spring).
- Years can be said two ways: "dix-neuf cent quatre-vingt-seize" or
  "mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-seize" for 1996 (both mean "nineteen
  ninety-six" style vs. "one thousand nine hundred..." style).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
Days (les jours): lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, samedi, dimanche
Months (les mois): janvier, février, mars, avril, mai, juin, juillet, août,
  septembre, octobre, novembre, décembre
Seasons (les saisons): le printemps, l'été, l'automne, l'hiver

VOCABULARY:
aujourd'hui - today
demain - tomorrow
hier - yesterday
la semaine - the week
le mois - the month
l'année (f.) - the year
quelle est la date ? - what is the date?

EXAMPLES:
Nous sommes le 14 août. - It's August 14th. (lit. "we are the 14 August")
Mon anniversaire est le premier mai. - My birthday is May 1st.
En hiver, il neige souvent. - In winter, it often snows.
Je pars en vacances au printemps. - I'm going on vacation in spring.

NOTES:
- To ask "what day is it," French uses "Quel jour sommes-nous ?" or
  "On est quel jour ?" — literally "what day are we."

PRACTICE:
Write today's date in French, then your birthday.

LESSON 7: Telling Time
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
French has both a casual 12-hour system (used in conversation) and the
24-hour "military" system (used for schedules, TV listings, transport).

GRAMMAR:
- "What time is it?" = Quelle heure est-il ? Answer with "Il est..."
  (It is...).
- On the hour: Il est trois heures. (It's 3:00.) Note "heure(s)" is always
  stated, unlike English which can drop "o'clock."
- Minutes past the hour: add the number of minutes directly:
  Il est trois heures dix. (3:10.)
- Half past: et demie — Il est trois heures et demie. (3:30.)
- Quarter past/to: et quart (quarter past), moins le quart (quarter to):
  Il est trois heures et quart. (3:15.) Il est trois heures moins le
  quart. (2:45.)
- Minutes before the next hour (past the half-hour, 12-hour system):
  count backward with "moins": Il est quatre heures moins dix. (3:50,
  lit. "four o'clock minus ten.")
- Noon/midnight have their own words: midi (noon), minuit (midnight) — no
  "heures" needed: Il est midi. (It's noon.)
- 24-hour clock (official/written use): simply state the hour 0–23 plus
  minutes, no "et quart"/"moins": 15h30 = quinze heures trente (3:30 PM);
  20h45 = vingt heures quarante-cinq (8:45 PM).
- "At what time" = à quelle heure: Le train part à quatorze heures. (The
  train leaves at 2:00 PM.)

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(time expressions, not a verb table)
Il est une heure. - It's 1:00.
Il est deux heures. - It's 2:00.
Il est midi / minuit. - It's noon / midnight.
...heures et quart / et demie / moins le quart - quarter past / half past /
  quarter to

VOCABULARY:
une heure - an hour / o'clock
une minute - a minute
tôt - early
tard - late
maintenant - now
en retard - late (for an appointment)
en avance - early (ahead of schedule)

EXAMPLES:
Il est huit heures moins vingt. - It's 7:40 (twenty to eight).
Le film commence à vingt heures. - The movie starts at 8:00 PM.
Je me lève à sept heures et demie. - I get up at 7:30.
Nous sommes en retard ! - We're late!

NOTES:
- In casual conversation, French speakers do sometimes switch between the
  12-hour and 24-hour styles, but printed schedules (trains, planes, TV)
  almost always use the 24-hour clock — get comfortable converting quickly
  (15h = 3 PM, subtract 12 for PM hours above 12).

PRACTICE:
Convert to French time phrases: 9:15 AM, 6:45 PM, 12:00 PM, 23h50.

================================================================
LESSON 8: Greetings, Introductions, and Polite Expressions
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
The essential social toolkit: how to greet people, introduce yourself,
choose between formal and informal address, and use the core polite
phrases that keep every interaction smooth.

GRAMMAR:
- Formal vs. informal "you": use "tu" with family, friends, children, and
  peers your own age in casual settings. Use "vous" with strangers, older
  people, in professional settings, or anyone you're not on familiar terms
  with. When unsure, default to "vous" — it's never rude to be too formal,
  but it can be rude to be too familiar.
- Greetings change with time of day: bonjour (hello/good day, used until
  evening), bonsoir (good evening), bonne nuit (good night — only when
  parting for the night/going to bed, not a greeting).
- "Comment allez-vous ?" (formal) / "Comment vas-tu ?" or "Ça va ?"
  (informal) = "How are you?" Common replies: "Je vais bien, merci."
  (I'm doing well, thanks.) or simply "Ça va." (I'm okay / So-so.)
- To introduce yourself: "Je m'appelle..." (My name is...) or "Je suis..."
  (I am...). To introduce someone else: "Je vous présente..." (formal) /
  "Je te présente..." (informal) (Let me introduce you to...).
- "Enchanté(e)" (Pleased to meet you) — agrees in gender with the speaker
  (a man says "enchanté", a woman says "enchantée").

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(social phrases, not a verb table)

VOCABULARY:
bonjour / bonsoir - hello / good evening
au revoir / à bientôt - goodbye / see you soon
comment allez-vous ? / ça va ? - how are you? (formal / informal)
je vais bien, merci - I'm well, thank you
s'il vous plaît / s'il te plaît - please (formal / informal)
merci beaucoup / de rien - thank you very much / you're welcome
pardon / excusez-moi - sorry / excuse me
enchanté(e) - pleased to meet you
monsieur / madame / mademoiselle - sir / ma'am / miss

EXAMPLES:
Bonjour, madame. Comment allez-vous ? - Hello, ma'am. How are you?
Salut ! Ça va ? - Hi! How's it going? (informal)
Je m'appelle Thomas, et vous ? - My name is Thomas, and you?
Enchantée de faire votre connaissance. - Pleased to make your acquaintance.
  (a woman speaking, formal register)

NOTES:
- "Salut" works both as "hi" and "bye" but only in informal contexts —
  never use it with someone you'd address as "vous."
- Address strangers as "monsieur"/"madame" even without a name — dropping
  it can sound abrupt in French, unlike English.

PRACTICE:
Write a short formal exchange (greeting, "how are you," introduce yourself)
and then rewrite it informally, changing vous->tu and adjusting vocabulary.
