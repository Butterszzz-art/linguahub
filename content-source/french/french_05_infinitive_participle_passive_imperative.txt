LESSON 31: The Infinitive Mood
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
The infinitive (the unconjugated "to do" form) is used far more often in
French than in English — as a subject, after prepositions, and after many
conjugated verbs, sometimes requiring a specific connecting preposition
that must be memorized verb by verb.

GRAMMAR:
- The infinitif présent is the dictionary form (parler, finir, vendre) and
  can function as a noun (Fumer est mauvais pour la santé. = Smoking is
  bad for your health. — no "-ing" gerund needed, unlike English).
- The infinitif passé ("past infinitive," to have done) is formed with
  avoir or être (in the infinitive) + past participle, used after certain
  expressions (especially "après") to show a completed action before the
  main verb: Après avoir mangé, il est parti. (After eating [lit. "having
  eaten"], he left.) Après être arrivée, elle a téléphoné. (After
  arriving, she called.)
- When one conjugated verb is followed by a second verb, the second verb
  is almost always in the infinitive — but the CONNECTING WORD varies by
  verb and must be memorized:
    No preposition needed (verb + infinitive directly): vouloir, pouvoir,
      devoir, aimer, préférer, savoir, espérer, aller...
      Je veux partir. (I want to leave.)
    Verb + À + infinitive: aider à, apprendre à, commencer à, continuer à,
      réussir à, s'habituer à...
      J'apprends à cuisiner. (I'm learning to cook.)
    Verb + DE + infinitive: décider de, essayer de, finir de, oublier de,
      arrêter de, avoir besoin de, avoir envie de, être content de...
      J'ai décidé de partir. (I decided to leave.)
- After a preposition other than "de," French always uses the infinitive
  where English uses "-ing": avant de partir (before leaving), sans
  parler (without speaking), au lieu de dormir (instead of sleeping).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(pattern reference, not a single verb)
infinitif présent: parler / finir / vendre
infinitif passé: avoir parlé / avoir fini / avoir vendu
                  être allé(e)(s) / s'être levé(e)(s)

VOCABULARY:
avant de - before (+ infinitive)
après (avoir/être...) - after (+ past infinitive)
sans - without
au lieu de - instead of
apprendre à - to learn to
décider de - to decide to
avoir besoin de - to need to

EXAMPLES:
Avant de partir, vérifie que la porte est fermée. - Before leaving, check
  that the door is closed.
Après avoir fini le travail, nous sommes allés au cinéma. - After
  finishing the work, we went to the movies.
Elle a réussi à obtenir le poste. - She succeeded in getting the job.
J'ai oublié de fermer la fenêtre. - I forgot to close the window.

NOTES:
- There's no shortcut for the à/de/no-preposition pattern — build a
  running list of verb + preposition combinations as you encounter them,
  and review it regularly.

PRACTICE:
Write three sentences using "avant de + infinitive" and three using
"après avoir/être + past participle."

LESSON 32: The Present Participle and the Gerund
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
French has an -ant form (like English "-ing") used in two related but
distinct ways: as a present participle (adjectival/verbal) and as a
gerund introduced by "en" (showing simultaneity or manner).

GRAMMAR:
- Formation: take the nous form of the present tense, drop -ons, add
  -ant: parlons -> parlant; finissons -> finissant; faisons -> faisant.
  Three irregular exceptions: être -> étant, avoir -> ayant, savoir ->
  sachant.
- The present participle (participe présent) can function like an
  adjective (agreeing in gender/number) or describe an action happening
  alongside the main verb without "en": Les enfants, courant dans le
  jardin, riaient fort. (The children, running in the garden, were
  laughing loudly.)
- The gerund (le gérondif) is "en" + present participle, and expresses:
    - simultaneity ("while doing"): Elle chante en conduisant. (She sings
      while driving.)
    - manner ("by doing"): Il a appris le français en regardant des
      films. (He learned French by watching movies.)
    - The gerund's subject must be the SAME as the main clause's subject.
- Important contrast with English: French normally does NOT use an -ant
  form after most conjugated verbs the way English uses "-ing" after
  verbs like "like/start/stop" — French uses the infinitive instead
  (J'aime lire, not "j'aime lisant" — see Lesson 31).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(formation reference)
parler -> parlant   finir -> finissant   vendre -> vendant
être -> étant   avoir -> ayant   savoir -> sachant

VOCABULARY:
en + [participe présent] - while/by (doing)
tout en + [participe présent] - all while (doing) — emphasizes
  simultaneity/contrast

EXAMPLES:
Elle regarde la télé en mangeant. - She watches TV while eating.
Il est devenu riche en travaillant dur. - He became rich by working hard.
Tout en sachant la vérité, il n'a rien dit. - While knowing the truth
  [even though he knew], he said nothing.
Ayant fini son travail, elle est sortie se promener. - Having finished her
  work, she went out for a walk. (present participle, no "en")

NOTES:
- Don't confuse the gerund (en + -ant, showing HOW/WHEN) with simply using
  the infinitive after most verbs — when in doubt about a verb + verb
  construction, the infinitive (Lesson 31) is far more often correct than
  the gerund.

PRACTICE:
Write three sentences describing how you do something using "en +
gérondif" ("I learn French by...", "I relax by...", etc.).

LESSON 33: The Passive Voice
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
The passive voice shifts focus from the doer of an action to the
receiver of the action ("the book was written by..." instead of "she
wrote the book"). French can form it directly but often prefers
alternatives.

GRAMMAR:
- Formation: être (conjugated in whatever tense is needed) + past
  participle (agreeing with the subject) + optionally "par" (by) + the
  agent: Le livre a été écrit par Victor Hugo. (The book was written by
  Victor Hugo.)
- The passive can be formed in any tense by simply changing the tense of
  être: Le livre est écrit (present passive) / était écrit (imparfait
  passive) / sera écrit (future passive), etc.
- French uses the passive LESS often than English — it can sound heavy or
  overly formal. Common alternatives:
    1. Use "on" + active verb (very common, especially in speech):
       On parle français ici. (French is spoken here.) instead of
       "Le français est parlé ici."
    2. Use a reflexive/pronominal verb for general truths: Ce plat se
       mange froid. (This dish is eaten cold.) instead of "est mangé."
    3. Simply flip to the active voice if the agent is known and
       important: Victor Hugo a écrit ce livre. (Victor Hugo wrote this
       book.)
- Only verbs that take a direct object (transitive verbs) can be made
  passive, and only the direct object can become the subject of the
  passive sentence — indirect objects cannot (unlike some English
  passives).

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(pattern reference)
Active:  Marie écrit la lettre. (Marie writes the letter.)
Passive: La lettre est écrite par Marie. (The letter is written by Marie.)

VOCABULARY:
par - by (introduces the agent)
on - impersonal "one/people/they" (common passive alternative)
se + verb - reflexive-as-passive for general truths

EXAMPLES:
La Tour Eiffel a été construite en 1889. - The Eiffel Tower was built in
  1889.
Ce vin se boit frais. - This wine is drunk chilled. (reflexive, natural
  alternative to passive)
On m'a dit que le magasin était fermé. - I was told that the shop was
  closed. (on + active, very natural way to express an English passive)
Les billets seront vendus demain. - The tickets will be sold tomorrow.

NOTES:
- When translating an English passive sentence into French, always ask
  first whether "on" or a reflexive verb would sound more natural before
  defaulting to the literal être + past participle construction.

PRACTICE:
Rewrite these passive English sentences using "on" in French: "French is
spoken in many countries." "The window was broken." "Dinner is served at
8."

LESSON 34: Indirect Speech
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
Indirect (reported) speech restates what someone said without quoting
them directly — "She said that..." This requires shifting pronouns,
tenses, and sometimes time expressions, following a set of predictable
rules called "la concordance des temps" (sequence of tenses).

GRAMMAR:
- Direct speech uses quotation marks and the exact original words: Elle a
  dit : « Je suis fatiguée. » (She said, "I'm tired.")
- Indirect speech drops the quotation marks, adds "que," and adjusts
  pronouns/possessives to fit the new speaker's perspective: Elle a dit
  qu'elle était fatiguée. (She said that she was tired.)
- Sequence of tenses (la concordance des temps) — when the main
  reporting verb (dire, penser, demander...) is in a PAST tense, the tense
  inside the "que" clause shifts back:
    présent -> imparfait:      "Je suis fatiguée" -> qu'elle était fatiguée
    passé composé -> plus-que-parfait: "J'ai fini" -> qu'elle avait fini
    futur simple -> conditionnel présent: "Je partirai" -> qu'elle
      partirait
    futur antérieur -> conditionnel passé: "J'aurai fini" -> qu'elle
      aurait fini
  If the reporting verb is in the PRESENT tense, no tense shift is needed.
- Time/place expressions often shift too: aujourd'hui -> ce jour-là;
  demain -> le lendemain; hier -> la veille; ici -> là.
- Reported YES/NO questions use "si" (if/whether): Il m'a demandé si
  j'étais prêt(e). (He asked me if I was ready.)
- Reported information questions keep the question word but drop
  inversion/est-ce que: Elle m'a demandé où j'habitais. (She asked me
  where I lived.) Note: "qu'est-ce que" becomes "ce que" in indirect
  speech: Il a demandé ce que je faisais. (He asked what I was doing.)

CONJUGATION TABLE:
(tense-shift reference — see grammar section above)

VOCABULARY:
dire que - to say that
demander si/ce que - to ask whether/what
répondre que - to answer that
le lendemain - the next day
la veille - the day before

EXAMPLES:
Direct: Il a dit : « Je viendrai demain. »
Indirect: Il a dit qu'il viendrait le lendemain. (futur -> conditionnel;
  demain -> le lendemain)
Direct: Elle m'a demandé : « Où habites-tu ? »
Indirect: Elle m'a demandé où j'habitais. (present -> imparfait, no
  inversion)
Direct: Ils ont demandé : « Est-ce que vous avez fini ? »
Indirect: Ils ont demandé si nous avions fini. (passé composé -> plus-
  que-parfait)

NOTES:
- This is one of the more mechanical grammar topics — build fluency by
  taking short dialogues you already know and rewriting them as indirect
  speech, tense by tense.

PRACTICE:
Convert to indirect speech: Elle a dit : « J'ai perdu mes clés et je ne
sais pas où elles sont. »

LESSON 35: The Imperative Mood
================================================================

OVERVIEW:
The imperative is used for commands, requests, and suggestions — it exists
only for tu, nous, and vous (no subject pronoun is stated).

GRAMMAR:
- Formation: take the present-tense tu/nous/vous form of the verb and
  drop the subject pronoun. For -er verbs (and verbs like ouvrir that
  conjugate like -er verbs), also drop the final -s from the tu form:
    parler: (tu) Parle ! / (nous) Parlons ! / (vous) Parlez !
    finir: (tu) Finis ! / (nous) Finissons ! / (vous) Finissez !
    vendre: (tu) Vends ! / (nous) Vendons ! / (vous) Vendez !
- Être, avoir, and savoir have irregular imperative forms (borrowed from
  the subjunctive stem): Sois sage ! (Be good!) / Ayons du courage !
  (Let's have courage!) / Sachez que... (Know that...).
- With reflexive verbs (Lesson 14): in the AFFIRMATIVE, the reflexive
  pronoun moves after the verb (hyphenated), and "te" becomes "toi":
  Lève-toi ! (Get up!) Asseyez-vous ! (Sit down!) In the NEGATIVE, normal
  word order is kept: Ne te lève pas ! (Don't get up!)
- With object pronouns, the same "flip" rule applies: in the affirmative,
  pronouns follow the verb, connected with hyphens (Donne-le-moi ! = Give
  it to me!); in the negative, they stay in normal position before the
  verb (Ne me le donne pas ! = Don't give it to me!).
- To soften a command into a suggestion, French often uses the
  conditional instead of a true imperative: Tu devrais te reposer. (You
  should rest.) is gentler than Repose-toi ! (Rest!)

CONJUGATION TABLE:
parler (to speak) — imperative:
(tu) Parle !     (nous) Parlons !     (vous) Parlez !
se lever (to get up) — imperative:
(tu) Lève-toi !   (nous) Levons-nous !   (vous) Levez-vous !
  negative: Ne te lève pas ! / Ne nous levons pas ! / Ne vous levez pas !

VOCABULARY:
Attention ! - Watch out!/Be careful!
Dépêche-toi ! / Dépêchez-vous ! - Hurry up! (informal/formal)
Écoute(z) ! - Listen!
N'oublie(z) pas de... - Don't forget to...
Aidez-moi, s'il vous plaît. - Help me, please.

EXAMPLES:
Ferme la porte, s'il te plaît. - Close the door, please.
Asseyez-vous et détendez-vous. - Sit down and relax.
Ne t'inquiète pas, tout ira bien. - Don't worry, everything will be fine.
Allons-y ! - Let's go!
Donnez-le-moi tout de suite. - Give it to me right away.

NOTES:
- "Allons-y !" (Let's go!) and "Vas-y !" / "Allez-y !" (Go on!/Go for it!)
  are extremely common everyday expressions built on the imperative of
  aller + the pronoun "y."
- Remember: aller's tu-form imperative keeps the -s only when followed by
  "y" for pronunciation reasons: "Va !" but "Vas-y !"

PRACTICE:
Write a short set of instructions (5 commands) for a recipe or a daily
routine, using tu-form imperatives.
