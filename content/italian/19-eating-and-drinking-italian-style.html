Eating and Drinking, Italian Style

Food culture in Italy runs on its own rhythm and rituals — especially around coffee. This lesson introduces the three daily meals, the drink vocabulary you'll use constantly, and the unwritten rules around ordering espresso like a local.

Grammar

  • Italians structure the day around three main meals: la colazione (breakfast), il pranzo (lunch), and la cena (dinner), with uno spuntino (a snack) possible in between.
  • The verb prendere (to take) does double duty for ordering food and drink, the way "to have" works in English: Prendo un caffè. (I'll have a coffee.) It conjugates as a regular -ere verb (Lesson 8).
  • The conditional form vorrei (I would like) is the polite, standard way to order anything — much softer than voglio (I want): Vorrei un cappuccino, per favore. You'll study the conditional formally later; for now, treat vorrei as a fixed polite phrase.
  • Quantities when ordering drinks use di (of): una bottiglia di... (a bottle of...), un bicchiere di... (a glass of...), mezzo litro di... (half a liter of...).

Examples

Prendo un caffè, grazie.

PREHN-doh oon kahf-FEH, GRAH-tsee-eh

I'll have an espresso, thanks.

Vorrei un cappuccino e una pasta.

vohr-RAY oon kahp-poot-CHEE-noh eh OO- nah PAHS-tah

I'd like a cappuccino and a pastry.

A che ora fai colazione?

ah keh OH-rah fahy koh-lah-TSYOH-neh

What time do you have breakfast?

Un bicchiere d'acqua naturale, per favore.

oon beek-KYEH-reh DAHK-kwah nah-too-RAH-leh, pehr fah-VOH-reh

A glass of still water, please.

Notes

  • Ordering "un caffè" always gets you an espresso — Italy has no concept of the oversized "coffee to go" cup, and asking for "un caffè grande" will likely get you a puzzled look.
  • Cappuccino is considered strictly a morning/breakfast drink; ordering one after roughly 11 a.m. — especially after a meal — marks you instantly as a tourist (more in Lesson 44).
