Demonstratives and Possessives

This lesson covers two families of words that point to and claim ownership of things: demonstratives (this/that) and possessives (my/your/ his/her). Both agree with the noun they modify, following patterns you've already seen with adjectives.

Grammar

  • Demonstrative adjectives/pronouns: questo/a/i/e (this/these) behaves like a regular Type 1 adjective (Lesson 6): questo libro, questa casa, questi libri, queste case.
  • Quello (that/those) is trickier — it borrows endings from the definite article system (Lesson 5) depending on the noun that follows: quel libro (like "il"), quello studente (like "lo"), quell'amico (like "l'"), quella casa (like "la"), quei libri (like "i"), quegli studenti (like "gli"), quelle case (like "le"). Used alone as a pronoun ("that one"), it simplifies to quello/a/i/e.
  • Possessive adjectives (my, your, his/her, our, your-pl, their) agree with the THING POSSESSED, not with the possessor — this is a genuine difference from English, where "his" and "her" depend on the owner's gender:
    il mio libro (my book) / la mia casa (my house) — same possessor,
    different endings based on what's owned
    Full set (masc. sg. shown): il mio, il tuo, il suo, il nostro, il
    vostro, il loro (their — invariable across gender/number, unlike the
    others)
  • Possessives normally keep the definite article (il mio libro), EXCEPT with singular, unmodified family members, where the article drops: mia madre (my mother), tuo padre (your father), sua sorella (his/her sister) — but the article returns if the noun is plural or modified: i miei genitori (my parents), la mia sorella maggiore (my older sister).

Examples

Questo libro è mio, quello è tuo.

KWEHS-toh LEE-broh eh MEE-oh, KWEHL- loh eh TOO-oh

This book is mine, that one is yours.

Mia madre e mio padre abitano a Torino.

MEE-ah MAH-dreh eh MEE-oh PAH-dreh AH-bee-tah-noh ah toh-REE-noh

My mother and father live in Turin.

I miei genitori sono in vacanza.

ee MYEH-ee jeh-nee-TOH-ree SOH-noh een vah-KAHN-tsah

My parents are on vacation.

Quella macchina rossa è la nostra.

KWEHL-lah MAHK-kee-nah ROHS-sah eh lah NOHS-trah

That red car is ours.

Notes

  • The dropped article with singular family terms is one of the most distinctive features of Italian possessives — mia madre, not "la mia madre" — and it's worth practicing as a fixed exception.
  • When quello functions as an adjective before a noun, it changes form like the definite article does; when it stands alone as a pronoun ("that one"), it simplifies back to the plain quello/a/i/e pattern.
