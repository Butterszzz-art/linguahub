Personal Pronouns in All Cases

You already know the German subject pronouns (ich, du, er, sie, es, wir, ihr, sie/Sie) from earlier lessons — those are simply the nominative forms. Just like the articles you've been drilling, pronouns change shape depending on case too. This lesson gives you the accusative and dative pronoun sets, which you'll need the moment a pronoun serves as an object rather than a subject.

Grammar

In English, only a few pronouns change between subject and object form (I→me, he→him, she→her, we→us, they→them); most stay identical. In German, nearly every pronoun changes, and — true to the pattern you've now seen three times — it changes differently depending on whether it's in the accusative or the dative.

A pronoun's gender always matches the grammatical gender of the noun it replaces, not the "real world" gender of the thing. Der Hund (dog, masculine) becomes er even though the dog's actual sex is unknown or irrelevant; die Brille (glasses, feminine) becomes sie.

Examples

Siehst du mich?

Do you see me? (accusative)

Ich liebe dich.

I love you. (accusative)

Er besucht uns.

He is visiting us. (accusative)

Gib mir die Flasche Wein, bitte.

Give me the bottle of wine, please. (dative)

Ich danke Ihnen.

I thank you. (formal, dative)

Notes

A fast way to check which set you need: ask whether the pronoun is a direct object (accusative — "sees whom?", "loves whom?") or an indirect object / following a dative verb or preposition (dative — "gives to whom?", "helps whom?", "thanks whom?"). This is exactly the same test you've already been applying to full nouns in Lessons 15 and 16 — pronouns just follow the identical logic in a shorter package.

Practice

  1. Replace "den Mann" with the correct accusative pronoun in: "Ich sehe den Mann."
  2. Replace "dem Mann" with the correct dative pronoun in: "Ich folge dem Mann."
  3. Translate: "Can you help me?" (helfen requires dative)
  4. Translate: "The flowers are for you." (für requires accusative)
  5. Explain why "sie" can mean "she," "they," or "her/them" depending on context and case.
