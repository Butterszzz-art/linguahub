The German Alphabet & Pronunciation

German spelling is far more predictable than English. Once you learn the sound each letter makes, you can read almost any German word aloud correctly — even one you've never seen. This lesson covers the alphabet, the four extra characters German adds to it, and the core vowel and consonant rules you'll lean on for the rest of the course.

Grammar

The German alphabet has the same 26 letters as English, plus four special characters:

  • ä, ö, ü — the umlauts. They are not decorations; they change both the sound and the meaning of a word. Mutter (MOO-ter) means "mother," but Mütter (MUE-ter) means "mothers."
  • ß — called the "eszett" or "scharfes S" (sharp S). It's pronounced exactly like an English "s" and appears after long vowels or diphthongs, e.g. Straße (street).

Vowel length is the single biggest pronunciation rule in German:

  • A vowel is long when followed by "h" (Stahl — steel), doubled (Teer — tar), or followed by a single consonant (Tag — day).
  • A vowel is short when followed by two or more consonants (Tanne — fir tree).

Key consonant differences from English:

  • "w" sounds like an English "v" — Wasser (VAH-ser, water)
  • "v" sounds like an English "f" — Vater (FAH-ter, father)
  • "j" sounds like an English "y" — ja (yah, yes)
  • "z" sounds like "ts" — Zeit (tsyt, time)
  • "s" at the start of a word before a vowel sounds like an English "z" — sagen (ZAH-gen, to say)
  • "ch" has two sounds: a soft, breathy sound after e/i/ä/ö/ü (ich — iH), and a harsher, throat-clearing sound after a/o/u (ach — ahH)
  • "sch" sounds like English "sh" — schön (shern, beautiful)
  • "sp" and "st" at the start of a word are pronounced "shp" and "sht" — sprechen (SHPREH-Hen), Stadt (shtaht)

Notes

Don't fight the umlauts — treat ä, ö, ü as their own letters from day one, not as "a, o, u with dots." If your keyboard can't produce them, the accepted substitutes are ae, oe, ue, and ss for ß (so Straße can be written Strasse). This matters for later lessons: mixing up müssen (must) and Muse would be an odd sentence indeed.

Practice

  1. Sound out and write the phonetic pronunciation for: Haus, Tür, groß, Mütter, schön.
  2. Explain in your own words why Tag has a long "a" but Tanne has a short one.
  3. Say aloud: ich mache (I make) and ach so (oh I see) — notice the two different "ch" sounds.
  4. Spell your own first name using German letter names as a mental exercise (you'll drill actual letter names with an instructor or app — for now, just notice which of your name's sounds would change in German).
  5. True or false: "w" in German is pronounced like the English "w" in "water." (Explain your answer.)
