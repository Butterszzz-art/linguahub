Introduction to the Four Cases

This is the lesson that unlocks the rest of German grammar. In English, word order alone usually tells you who's doing what to whom — "Dog bites man" and "Man bites dog" mean very different things purely because of order. German instead marks these roles directly on the noun's article (and sometimes the noun itself) using a system called case. There are four cases: nominative, accusative, dative, and genitive. This lesson gives you the big picture before Lessons 14–17 dig into each one individually.

Grammar

A case marks the grammatical role a noun plays in a sentence. German signals case primarily through the article attached to the noun, not through the noun's position, which is exactly why German word order can be so much more flexible than English — the case ending tells you who the subject is regardless of where it sits in the sentence.

The four roles, in brief:

  • Nominative — the subject, the one doing the action. Der Mann liest. (The man is reading.)
  • Accusative — the direct object, the thing being directly acted upon. Der Mann liest einen Roman. (The man is reading a novel.)
  • Dative — the indirect object, the person/thing something is done to or for. Der Mann gibt der Frau einen Roman. (The man gives the woman a novel.)
  • Genitive — shows possession, one noun belonging to another. Der Hut des Mannes. (The man's hat.)

Three factors determine which case a noun takes in a given sentence:

  1. Its grammatical role (subject = nominative, direct object = accusative, indirect object = dative)
  2. The specific verb — some verbs simply require a certain case regardless of the "logical" role. Most verbs take the accusative for their object, a smaller set of common verbs (helfen, danken, gehören, folgen) always require the dative.
  3. The preposition — many German prepositions force a specific case onto their object no matter what role that object would otherwise play. This is covered fully in Part 8, but you'll start noticing it immediately.

Examples

Der Mann liest.

The man is reading. (nominative — subject)

Der Mann liest einen Roman.

The man is reading a novel. (accusative — direct object)

Der Mann gibt der Frau einen Roman.

The man gives the woman a novel. (dative — indirect object; accusative — direct object, both in one sentence)

Der Hut des Mannes.

The man's hat. (genitive — possession)

Kann man das erkennen? Der Frau (dative) vs. die Frau (nominative)

spot the difference: der signals "to/for the woman," die signals "the woman [is doing something]."
