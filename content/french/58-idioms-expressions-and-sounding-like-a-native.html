Idioms, Expressions, and Sounding Like a Native

A closing set of favorite French expressions, common phrases that will make you sound more natural, and a few things to avoid saying — rounding off your practical toolkit.

Grammar

  • Idiomatic expressions often don't translate literally — learn them as fixed chunks rather than word-by-word: coûter les yeux de la tête (to cost an arm and a leg, lit. "to cost the eyes from the head"); poser un lapin à quelqu'un (to stand someone up, lit. "to put a rabbit on someone"); avoir le cafard (to feel down/blue, lit. "to have the cockroach").
  • A few phrases to be careful with: "Je suis plein(e)" does NOT mean "I'm full" (after a meal) in standard French the way it might sound — it can sound like claiming to be pregnant/drunk depending on context; the safer phrase is "J'ai bien mangé" or "Je n'ai plus faim" (I'm not hungry anymore).
  • Filler words/connectors that make speech sound more natural: "du coup" (so/as a result — extremely common in casual modern French), "en fait" (actually), "bref" (in short/anyway), "quoi" (informal sentence-ending filler, roughly "you know"), "voilà" (there you go / that's it).

Examples

Il pleuvait, du coup on est restés à la maison.

It was raining, so we stayed home.

Ça marche, on se voit à midi !

Sounds good, see you at noon!

Ce sac coûte les yeux de la tête !

This bag costs an arm and a leg!

Bref, la soirée s'est bien passée.

Anyway, the evening went well.

Notes

  • Sprinkling in a few of these connectors ("du coup," "en fait," "bref") after you've solidified core grammar is one of the fastest ways to make your spoken French sound noticeably more natural and less textbook-ish.

Practice

Write a short paragraph (5–6 sentences) about your day using at least three connector words from this lesson (du coup, en fait, bref, voilà).
