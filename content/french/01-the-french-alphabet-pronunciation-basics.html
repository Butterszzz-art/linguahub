The French Alphabet & Pronunciation Basics

French uses the same 26 letters as English, but many are pronounced very differently, and several sounds don't exist in English at all. This lesson builds the sound system you'll rely on for everything else.

Grammar

  • The alphabet (l'alphabet) is recited: a (ah), b (bay), c (say), d (day), e (euh), f (eff), g (zhay), h (ash), i (ee), j (zhee), k (kah), l (ell), m (emm), n (enn), o (oh), p (pay), q (kew), r (air), s (ess), t (tay), u (ew), v (vay), w (doobluh-vay), x (eeks), y (ee-grek), z (zed).
  • Vowel sounds: French vowels are pure and held steady (no gliding like English "day" -> "day-ee"). Key sounds:
    a -> "ah" (as in "papa")
    e -> "uh" (as in "le")
    é -> "ay" (as in "café")
    è/ê -> "eh" (as in "père")
    i -> "ee" (as in "ici")
    o -> "oh" (as in "mot")
    u -> pursed "ew" — round your lips as if for "oo" but say "ee" (this sound doesn't exist in English; contrast tu/tout)
    ou -> "oo" (as in "vous")
    oi -> "wah" (as in "moi")
    eu -> "uh" (rounded)(as in "peu")
  • Nasal vowels (air pushed through the nose, N/M not fully pronounced):
    an/am/en/em -> nasal "ah" (as in "dans", "temps")
    on/om -> nasal "oh" (as in "bon")
    in/im/ain/ein -> nasal "eh" (as in "vin", "pain")
    un/um -> nasal "uh" (as in "un" — merging with "in" in much of modern spoken French)
  • Consonants mostly match English, with exceptions:
    c -> "s" before e/i/y, "k" elsewhere (cinéma vs. cadeau)
    ç -> always "s" (garçon)
    g -> "zh" before e/i/y, hard "g" elsewhere (genou vs. gâteau)
    j -> always "zh" (jour)
    h -> always silent, but see Lesson 2 for mute vs. aspirate h
    r -> a soft guttural sound from the back of the throat (not the American r) — practice with "rouge", "Paris"
    ll -> usually "y" after i (fille = "fee-yuh"), but "l" elsewhere
    qu -> "k" (qui = "kee")
    th -> "t" (théâtre)
  • Final consonants are usually SILENT: petit -> "puh-tee" (t silent). Common exceptions: c, r, f, l are often pronounced (the mnemonic "CaReFuL" helps you remember which final consonants tend to be sounded).

Examples

Je m'appelle Marie.

My name is Marie. (practice the nasal "en" in "appelle")

Il fait beau.

The weather is nice. (practice silent final -t, nasal "ai")

Nous allons au marché.

We're going to the market. (practice "ou", nasal "on")

Notes

  • The single most common beginner mistake is pronouncing final consonants the way English does. When in doubt, drop the final letter unless it's c, r, f, or l.
  • The French "u" (tu, une, du) is the hardest sound for English speakers — round your lips tightly and try to say "ee" without moving your jaw.

Practice

Say these pairs aloud and notice the difference: tu / tout, dessous / dessus, bon / banc, vin / vent.
