Telling Time

French has both a casual 12-hour system (used in conversation) and the 24-hour "military" system (used for schedules, TV listings, transport).

Grammar

  • "What time is it?" = Quelle heure est-il ? Answer with "Il est..." (It is...).
  • On the hour: Il est trois heures. (It's 3:00.) Note "heure(s)" is always stated, unlike English which can drop "o'clock."
  • Minutes past the hour: add the number of minutes directly: Il est trois heures dix. (3:10.)
  • Half past: et demie — Il est trois heures et demie. (3:30.)
  • Quarter past/to: et quart (quarter past), moins le quart (quarter to): Il est trois heures et quart. (3:15.) Il est trois heures moins le quart. (2:45.)
  • Minutes before the next hour (past the half-hour, 12-hour system): count backward with "moins": Il est quatre heures moins dix. (3:50, lit. "four o'clock minus ten.")
  • Noon/midnight have their own words: midi (noon), minuit (midnight) — no "heures" needed: Il est midi. (It's noon.)
  • 24-hour clock (official/written use): simply state the hour 0–23 plus minutes, no "et quart"/"moins": 15h30 = quinze heures trente (3:30 PM); 20h45 = vingt heures quarante-cinq (8:45 PM).
  • "At what time" = à quelle heure: Le train part à quatorze heures. (The train leaves at 2:00 PM.)

Examples

Il est huit heures moins vingt.

It's 7:40 (twenty to eight).

Le film commence à vingt heures.

The movie starts at 8:00 PM.

Je me lève à sept heures et demie.

I get up at 7:30.

Nous sommes en retard !

We're late!

Notes

  • In casual conversation, French speakers do sometimes switch between the 12-hour and 24-hour styles, but printed schedules (trains, planes, TV) almost always use the 24-hour clock — get comfortable converting quickly (15h = 3 PM, subtract 12 for PM hours above 12).
