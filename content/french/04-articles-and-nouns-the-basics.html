Articles and Nouns — The Basics

Every French noun needs an article (or another determiner) in front of it almost always — French rarely drops the article the way English does. This lesson covers the core system; Part 6 goes deeper into tricky cases.

Grammar

  • Definite articles ("the"): le (m. sing.), la (f. sing.), l' (before a vowel/mute h, either gender), les (plural, either gender).
  • Indefinite articles ("a/an", "some"): un (m. sing.), une (f. sing.), des (plural, either gender).
  • Partitive articles ("some/any", for uncountable amounts): du (m. sing.), de la (f. sing.), de l' (before vowel/mute h), des (plural). Used for food/drink and abstract quantities: du pain (some bread), de la patience (some patience).
  • After a negation, un/une/des/du/de la generally become simply "de" (or "d'" before a vowel): J'ai un chien -> Je n'ai pas de chien.
  • Gender clues (not 100% reliable, but useful patterns):
    Often masculine: nouns ending in -age, -ment, -eau, -isme
    Often feminine: nouns ending in -tion, -sion, -té, -ette, -ure
  • Plural of nouns: add -s in writing (usually silent in speech) — un livre -> des livres. Nouns ending in -eau/-eu often take -x (un bateau -> des bateaux). Nouns ending in -s/-x/-z don't change (une souris -> des souris).

Examples

Je voudrais du pain et de l'eau.

I would like some bread and some water.

Elle a une sœur et un frère.

She has a sister and a brother.

Il n'y a pas de lait.

There isn't any milk. (partitive -> "de" after negation)

Les enfants aiment les bonbons.

Children love candy. (definite article used for general statements, unlike English)

Notes

  • Unlike English, French uses the definite article for general/abstract statements: "J'aime le café" = "I like coffee" (in general), not "I like THE coffee."
  • Gender must be learned with each noun — always learn "le/la + noun" together as a unit, never the noun alone.

Practice

Fill in the article: ___ (the) chat mange ___ (some) poisson. (Answer: Le chat mange du poisson.)
