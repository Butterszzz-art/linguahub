The Turkish Alphabet and Sounds

Turkish spelling is almost perfectly phonetic: every letter stands for exactly one sound, that sound never changes, and you always pronounce every letter in a word. This is very good news — once you know the alphabet, you can correctly pronounce any Turkish word you see, even one you've never encountered before. The Turkish alphabet has 29 letters: it's the same as the English alphabet but drops Q, W and X, and adds six extra letters (ç, ğ, ı, ö, ş, ü) not found in English.

Grammar

  • The Turkish alphabet: A a, B b, C c, Ç ç, D d, E e, F f, G g, Ğ ğ, H h, I ı, İ i, J j, K k, L l, M m, N n, O o, Ö ö, P p, R r, S s, Ş ş, T t, U u, Ü ü, V v, Y y, Z z.
  • Turkish vowels are always "short" — a Turkish u sounds like the u in the northern English "put," never like the oo-glide in "super." English speakers tend to draw Turkish vowels out too long, so aim for something crisp and short.
  • The vowels English also has:
    a - like the a in "cat" (northern English) or "cup" (southern English)
    e - like the e in "bet"
    i (with a dot, even capitalized: İ) - like the i in "sit"
    o - like the o in "hot"
    u - like the u in "put"
  • The vowels English does NOT have:
    ı (no dot, even capitalized as I) - like the "er" in "butter," or the u in "cousin" — a neutral, unstressed-sounding vowel
    ö - like the German ö, or the "eu" in French "deux" — round your lips as if to say "o" but say "e"
    ü - like the German ü, or the French u in "tu" — round your lips as if to say "u" but say "i" — a sound halfway between English "ee" and "oo"
  • Crucially: dotted İ/i and dotless I/ı are two completely different letters in Turkish, not capitalization variants of each other. This trips up almost every beginner, so it's worth fixing in your mind right away: İstanbul is spelled with a dotted İ, and its capital I keeps the dot.
  • Consonants that sound different from English:
    c - like the j in "John" (NOT like an English c)
    ç - like the ch in "child"
    j - like the s in "pleasure," or the j in French "Jacques"
    ş - like the sh in "show"
    v - a sound halfway between English v and w
    ğ (yumuşak g, "soft g") - has NO sound of its own. It always follows a vowel and lengthens that vowel instead — think of dağ (mountain) as "daa," or öğle (noon) as a lengthened "öö-leh." No Turkish word ever begins with ğ.
  • Unlike English, Turkish always pronounces every letter — there's no silent h (as in English "hour") and no dropped final r (as in non-rhotic English accents). Every letter earns its place.
  • Word stress: as a general rule, stress falls on the LAST syllable of a word (kapı - kah-PUH, "door"). There are exceptions — mostly place names and a handful of loanwords — which you'll meet as they come up.

Examples

Kapı büyük.

kah-PUH bew-YEWK

The door is big.

Çocuk güzel.

choh-JOOK gew-ZEL

The child is beautiful.

Bugün yağmur var.

boo-GEWN yah-MOOR var

There is rain today.

Öğretmen nerede?

ur-ret-MEN neh-reh-DEH

Where is the teacher?

Notes

Because Turkish spelling is so consistent, it's worth spending real time on this lesson before moving on — every other lesson in this course assumes you can sound out new words correctly on sight. If you're ever unsure how to pronounce a word later in the course, come back to this lesson's sound list. Keep an eye out especially for ı vs i and for ğ, since these are the two features English speakers most often get wrong at first.

Practice

  1. Read these words aloud, then check yourself against the phonetics: sokak (street), köpek (dog), balık (fish), üniversite (university), yaş (age).
  2. Circle the "extra" Turkish letters (not found in English) in this sentence: Öğrencinin çantası şurada.
  3. True or false: dotless ı and dotted i are just capital/lowercase forms of the same letter.
  4. Explain in your own words what the letter ğ does to the vowel before it.
  5. Which syllable is normally stressed in a Turkish word: first, middle, or last?
