Are You English? — Personal Pronouns and the Verb "To Be"

This lesson covers how Turkish handles am/is/are, since Turkish has no separate verb "to be" the way English does — instead, it attaches personal endings directly onto the noun or adjective. You'll also learn the personal pronouns, how to keep vowels apart with buffer consonants, numbers beyond ten, the three Turkish words for "this/that," and the core set of question words.

Grammar

  • Personal pronouns: ben (I), sen (you, informal singular), o (he/she/it), biz (we), siz (you, formal or plural), onlar (they). Siz is used for more than one person, or for a single person you're addressing formally — when in doubt, use siz.
  • The verb "to be" as endings: instead of a separate word, Turkish attaches the endings -im, -sin, (nothing), -iz, -siniz, -ler directly onto the noun or adjective. These endings follow four-way vowel harmony (so you'll see -im/-ım/-um/-üm, -sin/-sın/-sun/-sün, etc.), except for the "they" form -ler, which follows two-way harmony and is usually left out entirely.
    ben İngilizim - I am English
    sen İngilizsin - you are English
    o İngiliz - he/she is English (no ending at all)
    biz İngiliziz - we are English
    siz İngilizsiniz - you are English
    onlar İngiliz(ler) - they are English (the -ler is usually dropped) You usually drop the personal pronoun itself, since the ending already shows who you mean: İngilizim is enough to say "I am English." The exception is onlar (they) — you generally keep it, to avoid ambiguity.
  • Questions with "to be": split the word into the noun/adjective and its ending, then insert mı/mi/mu/mü between them.
    İngilizsiniz -> İngiliz siniz -> İngiliz misiniz? (Are you English?) Note the buffer -y- inserted before endings that start with a vowel: Güzelim (I'm beautiful) becomes Güzel miyim? (not "Güzel mim?").
  • Buffer consonants: Turkish never lets two vowels collide inside a word. When an ending starting with a vowel is added to something ending in a vowel, insert -y- to keep the pronunciation smooth (you'll meet the buffer -n- in a later lesson). Loanwords like maalesef ("unfortunately") are rare exceptions where two vowels do sit together.
  • Addressing people: use Bey (Mr) and Hanım (Mrs/Miss) AFTER a first name, not a surname: John Bey (Mr. Smith / literally "Mr. John"), Lucille Hanım. You can also attach Bey to a job title as a polite way to get someone's attention: şoför bey! (driver!), memur bey (officer). Use beyefendi/hanımefendi (or efendim for either) with strangers whose name you don't know.
  • Numbers beyond ten: yirmi (20), otuz (30), kırk (40), elli (50), altmış (60), yetmiş (70), seksen (80), doksan (90), yüz (100), bin (1,000), milyon (1,000,000), milyar (1,000,000,000). Build bigger numbers by simply stringing the pieces together: onsekiz (18), ikiyüzkırkbeş (245). Unlike English, Turkish doesn't say "a/one hundred" — just yüz.
  • Bu, şu, o ("this/that"): where English has two words, Turkish has three.
    bu - this (close to you)
    şu - that (nearby, but not in your hand)
    o - that (further away) The line between şu and o is a judgment call, not a hard rule.
  • Question words: kim? (who?), ne? (what?), nerede? (where?), ne zaman? (when?, literally "what time?"), nasıl? (how?), kaç tane? (how many?), ne kadar? (how much?, literally "what extent?").
  • The possessive endings -im (my) and -iniz (your): attach directly to a noun, following four-way vowel harmony. Ceketim güzel (My jacket is nice); Adınız ne? (What's your name?). If the noun ends in a vowel, use just -m or -nız.

Notes

Notice how impressed the immigration officer is in this unit's dialogue just because the traveller uses a few words of Turkish — that's a very common and genuine reaction you'll get in Turkey. Also note that siz can mean either "you-plural" or "you-singular-formal": if in doubt about which form to use with a stranger, siz is always the safe choice. Buyurun is a word you'll hear constantly in Turkey — it's a flexible, all-purpose prompt meaning roughly "here you are," "go ahead," or "what can I do for you?" depending on context.

Practice

  1. Give the "to be" form for each: (a) o + öğrenci (student) (b) biz + hazır (ready) (c) siz + açık (open).
  2. Turn Hazırız (We're ready) into a question.
  3. Translate using bu/şu/o appropriately: "This is nice" (something in your hand); "That's far away" (something on the horizon).
  4. Say the number 245 and the number 1900 in Turkish.
  5. Ask "What's your name?" and then answer "My name is ___."
