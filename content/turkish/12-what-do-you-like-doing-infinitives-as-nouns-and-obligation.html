What Do You Like Doing? — Infinitives as Nouns and Obligation

This lesson shows you how to use a verb's infinitive as if it were a noun — essential for talking about likes, dislikes, and abstract activities — and covers the several ways Turkish expresses obligation ("must," "should," "need to"). You'll also meet olmak's special idiomatic phrases and the noun-building ending -lik.

Grammar

  • Using infinitives as nouns: Turkish infinitives (the -mek/-mak dictionary form) can function as nouns, similar to English gerunds ("swimming is fun"). There are two versions: the "full" infinitive and a "short" version with the final -k dropped.
    Full infinitive — used with adjectives, with istiyorum, and with için: Türkçe öğrenmek kolay. - Learning Turkish is easy. Konuşmak istiyorum. - I want to talk. Oynamak için geldim. - I came in order to play.
    Short infinitive — used as an ordinary noun, including as the first
    half of a compound noun: konuşmak -> konuşma - conversation dondurmak -> dondurma - ice cream yüzme havuzu - swimming pool
    A subtle trap: the short infinitive can look identical to a negative
    command, distinguished only by stress. İÇ-me suyu! (Don't drink the
    water!) vs. iç-ME suyu (drinking water) — remember, negative verb
    forms stress the syllable right before -me.
  • Adding endings to infinitives used as nouns:
    -den attaches to the FULL infinitive: Uçmaktan korkuyorum. (I'm
    afraid of flying.)
    -i or -e attach to the SHORT infinitive: Pişirmeyi bıraktı. (He
    stopped cooking.) Koşmaya başladı. (He began to run.)
    Possessed endings also attach to the SHORT infinitive: Benim Türk
    olmam iyi. (It's good that I'm Turkish — literally "my being Turkish
    is good.") You can usually drop the personal pronoun (benim) here,
    though including it makes the meaning clearer.
  • Expressing obligation with lazım, gerek, şart, and mecbur: combine any of these words with the short infinitive (plus a possessed ending) to say what you need, should, or are forced to do.
    Saat sekizde işte olmam lazım. - I need to be at work at eight.
    Saat sekizde işte olması gereklidir. - She needs to be at work at
    eight. Şart and mecbur are the strongest of these — closer to "absolutely must." There's also a verb, gerekmek, useful for talking about obligation in the future or past: Onların işte olması gerekecek. (They will have to be at work.) Dün onların işte olması gerekti. (They had to be at work yesterday.)
  • Expressing obligation with -meli: spot must/should/ought to by -meli (two-way harmony) added to the verb stem, then the personal ending.
    Okula gitmelisin. - You must go to school.
    Girmemeliler. - They mustn't enter. -meli is a common way to give advice or make a strong suggestion: Doktora gitmelisiniz. (You must see a doctor.)
  • Olmak in idiomatic phrases: beyond "to be/become," olmak appears in many fixed expressions worth learning as-is:
    oldu - that's settled, that's fine olur mu? - is that OK?
    ne oluyor? - what's happening? olamaz - it's not possible
    olsun - let it be, forget it olabilir - it's possible
  • The noun-building ending -lik: attach to adjectives, adverbs, or nouns to create new nouns, similar to English "-ness":
    mutlu (happy) -> mutluluk (happiness) hasta (ill) -> hastalık
    (illness) It also creates nouns for objects/places (kimlik - identity card, from kim - who) and adjectives meaning "for" (iki kişilik - for two people, günlük - daily, from gün - day).

Examples

Deniz kenarında yürümeyi severim.

deh-NEEZ keh-nah-ruhn-DAH yew-rew-meh-YEE seh-veh-REEM

I like walking by the sea.

Futbol seyretmekten nefret ediyorum.

foot-BOHL sey-ret-mek-TEN nef-RET eh-dee-yoh-room

I hate watching football.

Saat sekizde işte olmam lazım.

sah-AHT seh-keez-DEH eesh-TEH ohl-MAHM lah-ZUHM

I need to be at work at eight.

Doktora gitmelisiniz.

dohk-toh-RAH geet-meh-lee-see-NEEZ

You must see a doctor.

Sigara içmeyi bırakmalısın.

see-gah-RAH eech-meh-YEE buh-rahk-mah-luh-SUHN

You must stop smoking.

Notes

Notice how Turkish repeats a word for emphasis rather than using an intensifying adverb — çok çok çalışıyorum ("I work very very much") is a completely normal and common way to stress a point, not childish or incorrect. Also, when this unit's speaker uses lazım, note that it's an Arabic loanword pronounced with a lengthened first vowel (LAA-zuhm) — a small exception to Turkish's normal short-vowel rule, flagged back in Lesson 2.

Practice

  1. Say "learning Turkish is easy" using the full infinitive.
  2. Turn pişirmek (to cook) into a short-infinitive noun and use it in "He stopped cooking."
  3. Express "I need to go to work" three different ways: with lazım, with gerek, and with -meli.
  4. Build a noun meaning "happiness" from mutlu using -lik.
  5. What's the difference in meaning (and stress) between İÇ-me suyu! and iç-ME suyu?
