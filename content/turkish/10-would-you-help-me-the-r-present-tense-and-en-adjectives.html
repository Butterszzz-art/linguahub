Would You Help Me? — The -r Present Tense and -en Adjectives

This lesson introduces Turkish's SECOND present tense — traditionally called the "wide" or aorist present — which is used very differently from -iyor: for promises, willingness, requests, habitual truths, and storytelling. You'll also learn to build adjectives out of verbs (the equivalent of English "-ing" adjectives like "the running man"), the "by...-ing" ending -erek, and more verbs that require a specific case ending on their object.

Grammar

  • The -r present tense: build it by adding -r, -ir, or -er to the verb stem (plus the personal "to be" ending). Which one you use depends on the stem:
    - stems ending in a vowel: just add -r. söylerim (I say)
    - one-syllable stems (most): add -er. geçerim (I pass)
    - one-syllable stems ending in -r or -l: add -ir. kalırım (I stay)
    - stems with more than one syllable: add -ir (four-way harmony). inanırım (I believe) There's no fully reliable rule for which one-syllable verbs take -er vs. -ir — these are best learned by encountering each verb individually.
  • Negative -r present forms: spot them by -mez/-maz. Build by adding -me to the stem, then -z (except for the "I" and "we" forms, which drop the z in statements but restore it in questions).
    almam - I don't take bilmezsin - you don't know
    bulmayız - we don't find bulmaz mıyız? - don't we find?
  • Uses of the -r present tense: this tense covers a cluster of related meanings quite different from -iyor:
    making promises: Sana dondurma alırım. - I'll buy you an ice-cream.
    willingness: Yardım ederim. - I'll help.
    intention: Yarın gelirim. - I'll come tomorrow.
    polite requests: Kapıyı açar mısınız? - Would you open the door?
    offers: Çay içer misiniz? - Would you like some tea?
    set phrases: Teşekkür ederim. - Thank you.
    telling stories/jokes: Üç erkek bara girerler... - Three men go into a bar...
    unchanging facts: Su 0°C altında donar. - Water freezes below 0°C.
    habitual actions: Sık sık plaja giderim. - I often go to the beach.
    uncertainty/hope: Belki gelir. - Maybe he'll come. Umarım, gitmezsiniz. - I hope you won't go.
  • -en adjectives (the "subject participle"): turn a verb into an adjective describing whoever performs the action, similar to English "-ing" adjectives. Add -en (two-way harmony) to the stem.
    koşan adam - the running man, the man who's running
    İstanbul'a giden tren - the train going to İstanbul You can also use -en adjectives without a noun, in which case they act like nouns meaning "the one(s) who...": Masada oturan güzeldi. (The one sitting at the table was pretty.) Add -ler to make them plural.
  • -erek ("by...-ing"): attach to a verb stem to describe how something was done.
    Yürüyerek geldim. - I came on foot (by walking).
    Gazeteye bakarak buldum. - I found it by looking at the newspaper. It can also just mean "-ing": Gülerek gitti. (She left smiling.) The -erek form of olmak is special and means "as": Programcı olarak çalışıyorum. (I work as a programmer.)
  • More verbs needing -e, -den, or ile instead of -i: continuing from Lesson 8, here are more examples to build your instinct for these:
    -e: sormak (to ask), yardım etmek (to help), vurmak (to hit)
    -den: hoşlanmak (to enjoy), korkmak (to fear), bahsetmek (to mention)
    ile: evlenmek (to marry) — Bir elektrikçiyle evlenecek. (She's going to marry an electrician.)

Notes

Words like yavrum ("dear") and teyze ("auntie") in this unit's dialogue are common, friendly forms of address used with strangers — but only when there's a recognizable age gap between the speakers (an older person using them toward a younger one, or vice versa as a sign of respect). Acaba ("I wonder," "by any chance") is a small word that makes a request noticeably more polite — sprinkle it into requests when you want to soften them.

Practice

  1. Choose -er or -ir and conjugate for "I": geçmek (one-syllable), almak (one-syllable ending in a vowel + stem consonant), konuşmak (multi-syllable).
  2. Make gelirim negative.
  3. Build an -en adjective from gelmek describing "the train coming from Vienna."
  4. Translate using -erek: "She left smiling."
  5. Which ending does the object of korkmak (to fear) need?
