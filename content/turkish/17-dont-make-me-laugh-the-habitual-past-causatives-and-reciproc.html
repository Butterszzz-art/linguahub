Part 5 · Narrative and Advanced Turkish · Lesson 17

Don't Make Me Laugh! — The Habitual Past, Causatives, and Reciprocals

This lesson covers how to talk about things you USED to do, how to make someone else do something (causative verbs), the important difference between a verb happening to itself versus being done to something else, how to say "each other," and how Turkish builds adverbs.

Grammar

  • The habitual past ("used to," -erdi): built the same way as the past conditional pattern you'll see again in Lesson 19 — take the -r present form, strip the personal ending, and add the past "to be" ending.
    içersiniz -> içer -> içerdiniz - you used to drink
    Araba sürerdim. - I used to drive.
    Çok çalışırdılar (or Çok çalışırlardı). - They used to work a lot. Negate normally, using the -mez/-maz negative -r present as the base: Araba sürmezdim. - I didn't use to drive.
  • Causative verbs ("to make/have someone do something"): Turkish adds an ending to the verb stem to show that the subject is CAUSING the action rather than doing it directly. The most common causative ending is -dir, but several variants exist (-t, -it, -ir, -ert, depending on the verb):
    ölmek (to die) -> öldürmek (to kill)
    gülmek (to smile) -> güldürmek (to make someone smile)
    hatırlamak (to remember) -> hatırlatmak (to remind)
    korkmak (to be afraid) -> korkutmak (to scare)
    düşmek (to fall) -> düşürmek (to drop)
    çıkmak (to go out) -> çıkarmak (to take out, take off clothing) There's no single universal rule for which variant a given verb takes — though verbs with more than one syllable that end in r, l, or a vowel tend to take -t. Learn each causative pairing as you meet it.
  • Transitive vs. intransitive — "stopping something" vs. "just stopping": English sometimes uses the same verb both ways ("I stopped" / "I stopped the car"), but Turkish generally needs two different verbs: the plain form for something happening to itself (intransitive), and the causative form for making it happen to something else (transitive).
    Durdum. - I stopped. (intransitive)
    Arabayı durdurdum. - I stopped the car. (transitive/causative)
    Uyan! - Wake up! (intransitive) Uyandır onu! - Wake him up!
    (causative) When deciding how to translate an English verb like "stop," "wake," or "grow" into Turkish, always ask: is the subject doing this to itself, or causing it to happen to someone/something else?
  • Each other — birbiri: this word means "each other" and changes form to match the subject: birbirimiz (we... each other), birbiriniz (you... each other), birbirleri (they... each other).
    Birbirimizi seviyoruz. - We love each other.
    Birbirinize yardım edin! - Help each other! Birbiri is always the object (or part of the object) of its sentence, never the subject.
  • The -iş ending as "each other": besides the noun-building use from Lesson 16, -iş (and its variants) attached directly to a verb stem can ALSO mean "each other" — this is called the reciprocal form.
    Yarın görüşürüz. - We'll see each other tomorrow.
  • Adverbs — when, where, and how: many Turkish adjectives double as adverbs with no change at all: İyi gidiyor. (It's going well.) Türkçeyi güzel konuşuyorsunuz. (You speak Turkish well.) You can also build adverbs with -ce (Yavaşça sür. - Drive slowly), or by simply repeating a word (yavaş yavaş - slowly; zaman zaman - now and then). Adverbs normally go right before the verb they describe, and when a sentence has more than one adverb, "when" words usually come before "where" or "how" words. A useful set of "when" adverbs: artık (now, from now on), henüz (not yet), hiçbir zaman (never), bazen (sometimes), sık sık (often), her zaman (always).

Notes

The transitive/intransitive distinction (durmak vs. durdurmak) is one of the more subtle points in this course, but it becomes intuitive quickly once you start noticing it: ask "is the subject affecting only itself, or affecting something else?" before choosing which form of a verb to use. It's a genuinely different way of thinking about verbs than English uses, where the same word (like "stop" or "break") often covers both roles.

Practice

  1. Say "I used to smoke" (sigara kullanırdım pattern) using içmek or kullanmak.
  2. Build the causative of gülmek (to smile) and use it in "Don't make me laugh."
  3. Choose the right verb: "I woke up" vs. "I woke him up" — uyanmak or uyandırmak?
  4. Translate "We love each other" using birbiri.
  5. Build an adverb from yavaş meaning "slowly, slowly" by repetition.
