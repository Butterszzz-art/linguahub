Have You Been Waiting Long? — The Past Tense and Telling Time

This lesson introduces the simple past tense — the tense you use for anything that happened and finished, including situations where English would use "have done." You'll also learn to tell the time on the hour and half-hour, build compound nouns, and recognize verbs that insist on a particular case ending for their object.

Grammar

  • The past tense of "to be": spot it by the ending -di (two-way harmony, so also -dı/-du/-dü). zenginim (I am rich) -> zengindim (I was rich). If the word ends in a vowel, insert a buffer -y-: İyiydim (I was good). Negate with değil plus these same endings: Mutlu değildim (I wasn't happy).
  • The simple past tense of verbs: take the stem, then add the past "to be" ending directly.
    geldim - I came güldün - you smiled seyretti - he watched
    gittik - we went başladınız - you started beklediler - they
    waited Negate by inserting -me- before the past ending: gelmedim (I didn't come). Form questions by adding mı/mi/mu/mü AFTER the personal ending (note this is different from the -iyor tense, where the question particle splits the verb and the "to be" part): geldim mi? (did I come?), gelmedim mi? (didn't I come?)
  • Using the past tense for "have done": Turkish has no direct equivalent of the English "have done" tense — you simply use the simple past instead.
    Hiç rakı içtin mi? - Have you ever drunk rakı?
    Türkiye'ye gitmedim. - I haven't been to Turkey. Conversely, Turks often use the PAST tense in conversation where English would use the present: Geç kaldım (literally "I was late") is used to mean "I'm late"; Anladım ("I understood") means "I understand"; Geldim! ("I came!") means "I'm coming! I'll be right there!"
  • Telling the time (hours and half-hours):
    Saat kaç? - What's the time? Saat üç. - It's three o'clock.
    Saat kaçta? - At what time? Saat üçte. - At three o'clock.
    Saat dört buçuk. - It's half past four. One oddity: half past twelve is simply yarım, not "on iki buçuk."
  • Compound nouns: when you describe one noun with another (like "cheese sandwich" in English), the second noun takes the possessed ending -i/-si you learned in Lesson 7.
    pizza evi - pizza house yatak odası - bedroom
    Atatürk Havalimanı - Atatürk Airport Remember su (water) is irregular, taking -yu: portakal suyu (orange juice), maden suyu (mineral water).
  • Verbs that need -e or -den instead of -i: not every verb's object takes the direct-object -i ending. Some verbs — including bakmak (to look at), aşık olmak (to fall in love with), and binmek (to get into/onto) — insist their object take -e instead:
    Melek arabaya baktı. - Melek looked at the car. Others — including bıkmak (to get fed up with), inmek (to get out of), and nefret etmek (to hate) — insist on -den:
    Melek arabadan indi. - Melek got out of the car. There's no rule to predict which verbs behave this way; you simply learn each one's required ending as you meet it (dictionaries mark them, e.g. binmek (-e)).
  • İçin ("for," "for the sake of," "because of"): when için refers to a personal pronoun, give the pronoun a possessor ending first: benim için (for me), Senin için aldım (I bought it for you). When it refers to a plain noun, no ending is needed: Bu bira Ahmet için (This beer is for Ahmet). An alternative way to say "for" is simply the -e ending: Bu bira Ahmet'e (same meaning).
  • Question word combinations: you can build extra question words by adding endings or için to ne (what) or kim (who): neden/niçin/niye (why?), nerede (where?), neyin (of what?), kimin (whose?), kim için (who for?).
  • Word order with mı and de: place mı or de (meaning "also") directly after the specific word they refer to, since where you put them changes the meaning: Bana bira getiriyor musun? (Are you getting me a beer?) vs. Bana bira mı getiriyorsun? (Is it BEER that you're getting me?)

Examples

Nasılsınız? Teşekkür ederim, çok iyiyim.

nah-suhl-suh-NUHZ? teh-shek-KEWR eh-deh-REEM, chohk ee-YEE-yeem

How are you? Thank you, I'm very well.

Çok beklediniz mi? Çok beklemedik.

chohk bek-leh-dee-NEEZ mee? chohk bek-leh-meh-DEEK

Have you been waiting long? We haven't waited long.

Saat kaç? Saat üç.

sah-AHT kach? sah-AHT ewch

What's the time? It's three o'clock.

Yemeğinizi bitirdiniz mi?

yeh-meh-ee-nee-ZEE bee-teer-dee-NEEZ mee

Have you finished your meal?

Örümceklerden korkuyor.

ur-rewm-jek-ler-DEN kohr-koo-YOHR

He's afraid of spiders.

Notes

In Turkish, the most common reply to Nasılsınız? ("How are you?") is simply teşekkür ederim ("thank you") — there's no need to add "fine" or "well" the way English does. Also note how iyiydim inserts a buffer -y- despite there being no actual vowel clash — this is simply a fixed feature of the "to be" past tense after a vowel-ending word, worth memorizing as an exception.

Practice

  1. Put into the past tense: gel- (I), gül- (you), seyret- (he).
  2. Make a past-tense question: "Did you wait?" and "Didn't you wait?"
  3. Say "half past four" and "half past twelve" in Turkish.
  4. Build a compound noun for "orange juice."
  5. Which case ending does the object of bakmak (to look at) require — -i, -e, or -den?
