Is There Any Bread? — Greetings, Var/Yok, and Basic Questions

This lesson covers your very first real Turkish grammar: greetings and farewells, how to make a noun plural, how to turn a statement into a yes/no question, how to say "there is/there isn't," and how to count to ten. By the end, you'll be able to walk into a shop, greet the shopkeeper, ask whether they have something, and describe it with a simple adjective — which is exactly the situation this lesson's dialogue is built around (a tourist buying bread, sausage, and olives at a small grocer's).

Grammar

  • Greetings and farewells:
    günaydın (good morning) - only used in the morning
    iyi günler (good day) - a flexible daytime greeting or farewell
    iyi akşamlar (good evening) - from late afternoon onwards
    merhaba (hello) - safe at any time of day
    iyi geceler (good night)
    hoşça kalın (goodbye) - safe at any time, and the easiest to remember
  • Plurals: Turkish nouns are made plural by adding -ler or -lar (two-way vowel harmony — see Lesson 2). Choose whichever rhymes with the last vowel of the word: -ler after e-type (front) vowels, -lar after a-type (back) vowels.
    gün (day) -> günler (days)
    akşam (evening) -> akşamlar (evenings) Turkish sometimes uses a plural where English uses a singular set phrase (iyi günler, "good day," is literally "good days"). Conversely, Turkish uses a SINGULAR noun after a number, where English uses a plural: iki gün (two days), not "iki günler."
  • The question particle mı/mi/mu/mü: to turn a statement into a yes/no question, add this particle as a separate word after the item being questioned. It follows four-way vowel harmony (Lesson 2), matching the last vowel of the preceding word.
    İskoçyalı mı? - Is he Scottish?
    İstanbul mu? - Is it İstanbul?
    Türk mü? - Is he Turkish?
  • Var and yok: var means "there is/are," and yok means "there isn't/aren't." These two words are among the most useful in the whole language.
    Ekmek var mı? - Is there any bread?
    Çay var. - There is tea.
    Problem yok! - No problem!
  • Değil (negation): to make a simple statement negative, place değil after the noun or adjective you want to negate.
    Salam değil, sucuk. - It's not salami, it's garlic sausage.
    Hava sıcak değil. - The weather isn't hot. Tag onto any statement with Değil mi? to mean "isn't it? / aren't they?" — functioning much like French n'est-ce pas?
    Bu sucuk güzel, değil mi? - This garlic sausage is nice, isn't it?
  • Adjectives simply sit before the noun they describe, with no agreement needed (unlike French or Italian, Turkish adjectives never change form for gender or number): güzel elma (nice apple), güzel elmalar (nice apples) — güzel stays exactly the same either way.
  • Numbers 0-10: sıfır, bir, iki, üç, dört, beş, altı, yedi, sekiz, dokuz, on.

Notes

This unit's opening dialogue is monosyllabic on purpose — the book's point is that you can get remarkably far in Turkish using nothing but var, yok, ne, and mı/mi/mu/mü! Also worth knowing: it's polite in Turkey to say no simply through body language — raising your eyebrows with a single soft "tut" sound — rather than a spoken hayır. And don't be thrown by sentences like Salam değil, sucuk (literally "not salami, garlic sausage") — Turkish is comfortable leaving out words like "it" or "is" that English insists on.

Practice

  1. Turn these statements into questions: (a) Bu Türk. (b) Kahve sıcak.
  2. Make these nouns plural: gece (night), televizyon, polis, Türk.
  3. Answer with var or yok as if you were the shopkeeper: Süt var mı? (assume there is milk); Peynir var mı? (assume there is no cheese).
  4. Translate: "The weather isn't cold, is it?"
  5. Count aloud from sıfır to on.
